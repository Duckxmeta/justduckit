Elizabeth Melnick at Elizabeth’s Wildlife Center in Abbotsford has been taking the same call all morning. Someone found a goose on their deck. Someone else found one on the side of the road. The bird is either dead or dying. It is usually a cackling goose. Sometimes a snow goose. Sometimes a duck.

“This is the worst year we’ve had,” Melnick told CBC. “Absolutely the worst. [Wild birds] are just littered all over the place.” The center is averaging about 60 calls a day. They cannot keep up. The gloves, gowns, and masks they need for anything that might be flu are running short.

Down the road in Burnaby, the Wildlife Rescue Association of B.C. is seeing the same pattern. Jackie McQuillan, the program’s director, said the center has had a dramatic increase in calls from people who found sick waterfowl and an uptick in admissions where avian flu is the suspected reason.

This is the Pacific flyway doing what it does every fall. Birds move south. Some stop in the Fraser Valley to refuel on the mix of estuary and farmland. Some carry the virus. Some drop.

### Farms still clear

The B.C. Poultry Emergency Operations Centre says no infections have been reported on commercial farms so far this fall. Shawn Hall, the centre’s director, said they are sitting at the highest alert level they use — red biosecurity. Commercial poultry is under an indoor order. Farm gates stay closed. Essential vehicles get their tires cleaned. Personal protective equipment is mandatory. Air filtration is tighter.

“Unfortunately, we have a lot of experience dealing with this virus,” Hall said. Since 2021, British Columbia has been the hardest-hit province. More than 10 million domestic birds have been affected.

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency confirms there are currently no detections of highly pathogenic avian influenza in domestic birds in B.C. Officials remain cautious. The virus has not gone away. It is still moving in wild birds, and Washington State next door has seen recent cases in wild birds and dairy herds.

### Late arrivals

B.C.’s deputy chief veterinarian, Dr. Chelsea Himsworth, noted that the migrations are arriving “quite late” compared with what is expected. One hypothesis is that warmer temperatures farther north, tied to the El Niño pattern, are holding birds longer before they move south.

Himsworth said the province is seeing fewer birds on the landscape and fewer sick or dead birds submitted to the monitoring program than at this time last year. Whether that means a lower case load or just a delayed one is still unknown. “Time will tell.”

Provincial monitoring includes testing dead wild birds and sampling feces and sediment in specific wetlands. Those sediment samples from the Fraser Valley have been one of the better predictors of when the virus is likely to spill into domestic flocks. The information gets passed to farmers so they can tighten practices before a case appears.

### What the calls actually look like

A Postmedia editor in Langley watched an eagle swoop low over her house and then found an injured cackling goose on her second-story deck. A wildlife volunteer told her it was not the first call that day about a bird falling out of the sky. Birds with the flu can become dizzy and disoriented. They crash.

The center cannot take those birds. The risk to other animals and people is too high. Protocol is to leave the bird alone, take a photo or video, and report it to the provincial wild bird mortality hotline. Later that day a provincial employee collected the goose — already dead, double-bagged, wearing a raincoat and rubber gloves. He told the homeowner he already had two or three other dead birds in the car.

Melnick said the volume is beyond what the center can absorb. Resources are thin. The same supplies needed for safe handling of possible flu cases are the ones that run out first.

### Why this matters on the ground

For anyone running a sanctuary or keeping waterfowl, the pattern is familiar. The virus lives in wild birds. It moves with migration. It shows up first as phone calls and dead birds on the ground, then as tighter biosecurity rules, then as hard decisions about which animals you can take and which you have to turn away.

The indoor order and red alert on commercial farms are the industrial version of the same problem a smaller operation faces when the calls start stacking up. You do not get to choose when the birds arrive. You only get to decide how you handle what shows up.

Himsworth’s observation about late arrivals is the piece that keeps operators up. A quiet week does not mean the virus skipped the flyway. It can mean the birds are still north and the numbers will land later, all at once. Or it can mean this year is lighter. The monitoring program is built to tell the difference. The centers taking the calls do not have that luxury. They answer the phone.

Public guidance is straightforward: do not touch dead or sick birds. Report them to the hotline. Keep dogs and other pets away from waterfowl and their droppings. Backyard poultry keepers are being told to stay especially careful.

The centers in Abbotsford and Burnaby are the early warning. Sixty calls a day is not a normal fall. It is the flyway writing a report that the rest of us have to read.
