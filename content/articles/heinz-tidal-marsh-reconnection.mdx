Work is underway at John Heinz National Wildlife Refuge outside Philadelphia. Crews are excavating and dredging roughly 13,000 linear feet of tidal channels so Henderson Marsh can talk to Darby Creek again.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

That is 150 acres of freshwater tidal marsh that spent decades cut off by dikes, fill, and old construction. The goal is simple on paper and hard in the mud: put the tide back where the birds and the fish already know it should be.

## What the machines are actually doing

Michels Construction is the contractor on the channel work. They are dealing with a tidal wetland that moves under you, so the equipment and the schedule have to match the water. Spencer Madden, their senior project manager, put it plain: the job is to get the channels dug, get the tidal influence in, and open the place up.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

Ducks Unlimited regional biologist Ben Langey said the point is restoring the natural processes that let a tidal freshwater marsh function. Reconnecting Henderson Marsh to Darby Creek improves habitat for fish, waterfowl, wading birds, and everything else that uses the edge, while giving the marsh a better chance to store floodwater when the storms come.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

The species list is not theoretical. American black ducks and mallards are expected to use the restored feeding and resting habitat. Shorebirds and wading birds get the shallows. American shad and American eels get better movement between the creek and the marsh. That is the same short list we watch on the farm when the water level drops or a predator shows up: the birds that need shallow water and the ones that need a way out.

John Heinz is the nation’s first urban national wildlife refuge, established in 1972, and it holds the largest remaining freshwater tidal marsh in Pennsylvania. It sits minutes from the airport, inside a landscape of pavement, dikes, and neighborhoods that flood. The marsh has been altered for a long time. Historic filling and the construction of dikes restricted tidal movement and let invasive vegetation take hold. This project is one step toward reversing that.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

## Why a builder who runs a sanctuary cares

We run a sanctuary in rural Tennessee. Our birds are domestic. They do not migrate. They do not need tidal channels. But the math is the same: habitat is infrastructure. Water control, access, cover, and the ability to move when conditions change are what keep animals alive and keep the people who care for them from burning out.

Public land that still functions as waterfowl habitat is not automatic. It takes partners, money, specialized equipment, and people who understand that a dike can be both a problem and a tool. NOAA and other federal habitat restoration programs are in the funding mix, alongside the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and local partners. That is the kind of stacked, multi-year funding that lets a project move from design to dirt instead of staying on a wish list.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

The flood piece matters too. Eastwick and Tinicum Township have taken repeated hits from Darby and Cobbs creeks. Healthy tidal wetlands store and slow water. Restoring the marsh is wildlife work and neighborhood work at the same time. That dual use is what keeps these projects alive when budgets get thin. Pure “save the ducks” stories get attention. Stories that also keep houses dry get permits and partners.

## The longer view

This is part of a larger multiphase effort. Additional planning looks at restoring tidal flow to other parts of the refuge’s impoundment and at flood resilience across the Lower Darby Creek watershed. Visitor improvements—trails, signage, places to learn—are in the mix so the people who already use the refuge can see the change.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

For us, the lesson is practical. When public agencies and private conservation groups actually put steel in the ground on a marsh that birds still use, it is worth paying attention. It is not a press release about future potential. Crews are digging channels right now. The tide is going to move differently on 150 acres that sit inside one of the most built-up landscapes in the Northeast.

We know what it costs to keep water in the right place on a small farm. Scaling that knowledge to an urban refuge with federal partners and a construction crew that understands tidal ground is the difference between a sanctuary that survives and habitat that disappears under the next subdivision or the next flood. The birds do not care about the paperwork. They care whether the water is there when they need it.

That is the work. Dig the channels. Let the tide back in. Keep the marsh useful for the animals that still show up and for the people who live next to it.
