A volunteer walked the path at Broughshane Waterfowl Park on a Thursday morning in late October and smelled kerosene before he saw the black swans. They were coated. The pond below the collection birds was already contaminated. By the time the Northern Ireland Environment Agency got the call, oil absorbent booms were going in, but the damage was done.

Thirteen birds are dead. More than twenty others went to the USPCA for treatment. The park in County Antrim is closed while the cleanup continues. Chairman Gary Hayward called it a nightmare. The birds that died included two Trumpeter swans, more than ten ducks, and later a male black swan. Some of them the volunteers had known for years.

The source was a nearby home heating oil tank. Police and the local MLA have described it as vandalism or an oil theft that let the excess run into the storm drains. Flooding the night before had already put water over the pens, so the birds had nowhere to go when the kerosene arrived. Protective booms could not stop it. The combination turned a contained problem into a full contamination of the lower pond.

## What the rescue looked like on the ground

Volunteers worked through stormy weather to pull birds out of the affected pens. Three pens held five swans plus ducks and geese. The USPCA took the bulk of them for 24-hour care. A local clinic, Clare Vets, took a black swan and eight ducks overnight at no charge, washed them, and kept them warm. One member of the public saw the emergency and bought towels. The work was immediate and physical: catch, bag, transport, wash, dose charcoal for the ingested toxins, then watch.

Siobhan Hawthorn at the USPCA said the birds that arrived late had already taken in too much. Birds hide illness. You can bathe them twice and still not know what the next day will show. Most of the collection birds are non-native, so they cannot go back until the pond is fully cleared. That is the constraint. The waterway has improved, but the agency says further work continues as long as it is needed. Hayward has said it will be weeks. One local representative put the closure at five to six weeks.

## Why this is the ordinary failure mode for a waterfowl site

Broughshane is a community park that has been running for about thirteen years. It holds roughly 120 collection birds plus whatever wild birds use the water. It sits downstream of village drainage. One act on a heating tank, plus overnight flooding, was enough to shut the place and kill animals the volunteers treat as residents.

We run a sanctuary in rural Tennessee. The failure modes are different in detail and identical in kind. A storm can put a pond over its banks. A neighbor’s chemical drum can leak. A vandal can open a gate or cut a fence. The birds do not get to negotiate the drainage map. The people who show up at 6 a.m. to feed them do. When the call comes, the first hours are catching, washing, and deciding which ones can still be saved. The next weeks are the less visible part: the pond has to be clean enough that returning the survivors does not kill them a second time, and the volunteers have to keep doing the ordinary work while the site is closed and the bills keep arriving.

The Broughshane Trust has already said the park is a community asset. Donations and offers of help came in after the first reports. That is the other ordinary pattern. The public that walks the paths on a normal weekend shows up when the place is in trouble. The same public can also be the source of the next spill if the drainage path stays open. The MLA has been in contact with the agriculture and environment minister about preventing a repeat. No specific new safeguards have been announced yet.

## The part that does not show up in the photos

The photos that circulated show the black swan lying in the mud, feathers matted and glossy with oil. That image is accurate. What it does not show is the staff at the USPCA describing the emotional load of treating animals that will not all survive, or the volunteers who have already lost birds they had named and watched for years. It does not show the practical constraint that non-native collection birds cannot be released until the water is certified clean. It does not show the five-to-six-week closure window and what that does to a small attraction that depends on visitors.

For anyone who keeps waterfowl or runs a sanctuary, the sequence is familiar enough to be useful. The overnight flood plus the oil is a reminder that the drainage path into your water is a single point of failure. The charcoal doses and the second baths are the standard response once the birds are in hand. The longer constraint is the pond itself. Until it is clean, the survivors stay in care and the site stays closed. That is the cost that continues after the first rescue day ends.

The police have asked for information. The Trust has asked people to understand what happens when someone vandalizes a heating tank. Both requests are reasonable. Neither one restores the birds that are already gone or shortens the weeks the park will stay shut while the oil is removed.

We will keep watching for the reopening date and for any concrete changes to how the drainage into the park is protected. Until then the numbers stay the same: 13 dead, more than 20 in treatment, a park that has been closed since 22 October, and a cleanup that is still running.
