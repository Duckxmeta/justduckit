On November 20, Delaware wildlife and agriculture officials reported the state's first highly pathogenic avian influenza detections of the fall. The findings came from routine sampling of ducks taken during the opening segment of the 2026-27 duck season and from a commercial poultry flock in Sussex County. For anyone who keeps waterfowl or farm birds, or who hunts them, the details matter more than the headline.

The wild-bird cases involved seven ducks of three species—green-winged teal, American black duck, and northern shoveler—sampled at Little Creek Wildlife Area in eastern Kent County. USDA-APHIS collected the samples for DNREC's Division of Fish and Wildlife during the October 31 to November 7 season segment. Tests at the Pennsylvania Veterinary Laboratory returned presumptive positives for H5. Officials noted this differs from the pattern Delaware has seen in recent years, where large winter flocks of snow geese have been the primary wildlife hosts.

The same day, the Delaware Department of Agriculture announced a presumptive positive for H5 HPAI in a commercial poultry flock in Sussex County. The premises was quarantined and the birds are being depopulated. Those birds will not enter the food system. Officials called it the first detection in a contained private flock during the 2026-27 waterfowl migratory season.

## What the numbers and timing show

The detections arrived as the next segment of Delaware's waterfowl season opens November 25. DNREC asked hunters and the public to report groups of sick or dead wild birds—ducks, geese, shorebirds, raptors—through the Division of Fish and Wildlife's online form. Reporting does not guarantee a response to every case; it feeds the surveillance picture.

CDC guidance continues to describe the risk to the general public from H5 infections as low. Most human cases in recent years have been among people who work closely with infected poultry or dairy cattle. For hunters, the practical steps are the same ones wildlife agencies have repeated for years: do not handle obviously sick or dead birds, wear gloves when cleaning game, keep uncooked meat separate, and cook poultry to an internal temperature of 165 °F. Anyone who raises poultry and also hunts waterfowl is advised to treat footwear, clothing, and equipment as potential vectors and to keep domestic flocks away from areas wild birds can access.

## Why this lands on a sanctuary or farm

Highly pathogenic avian influenza moves through contact with infected birds, their secretions, manure, or contaminated equipment and clothing. Wild waterfowl can carry the virus without always showing obvious signs, which is why surveillance on hunter-harvested birds is useful. A commercial or backyard flock that shares air, water, or equipment with wild birds, or that receives visitors who have been around wild birds, sits closer to the transmission path.

For a small sanctuary or homestead in Tennessee or anywhere else in the flyway, the concrete moves are biosecurity rather than new equipment lists. Keep domestic waterfowl and poultry separated from wild birds where possible. Quarantine new arrivals. Change or clean boots and tools before moving between groups. Watch for sudden deaths, respiratory signs, or drops in egg production and report them. The Delaware commercial flock response—quarantine and depopulation—shows how quickly an agency moves once a presumptive positive is in hand. Smaller operations do not have the same resources, which is why the early wild-bird signal is useful.

## The next segment of the season

Delaware's next waterfowl season segment opens November 25. Officials are treating the wild-bird detections as a reason for heightened vigilance rather than a reason to close hunting. The commercial case is being handled through standard poultry response protocols. Both are the first of their kind for this fall in the state.

Anyone running birds along the Atlantic flyway can treat the Delaware reports as a local confirmation that the virus is circulating in migratory waterfowl again this season. The species list—teal, black ducks, shovelers—rather than only snow geese is the part worth noting. Surveillance continues. Reporting forms are open. The practical work on the ground is the same as it was last season: separate, clean, watch, and call it in when something looks wrong.
