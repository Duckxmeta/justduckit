Oregon wildlife officials put out a clear notice this week. Reports of highly pathogenic avian influenza are climbing among the ducks and geese moving through the Willamette Valley right now. Cackling geese, the smaller cousin of the Canada goose, are taking the heaviest hits.

This is not a background hum. It is the seasonal spike that shows up when waterfowl pack the Pacific Flyway in the fall. ODFW is seeing more sick and dead birds, most of them concentrated in the valley. Typical signs in wild birds: lethargy, inability to fly, swimming in circles, head shaking, cloudy eyes, loss of coordination. Some die within a couple of days of showing symptoms. Some ducks can carry and shed the virus without looking sick at all.

The agency is telling people to leave sick birds alone. Do not capture them. There is no effective treatment for wild or domestic birds once they are infected, and rehab centers cannot take them. Keep pets away. If you have to dispose of a carcass, gloves, a mask, and double-bagging before the trash is the practical advice they are giving. Large groups of dead birds can be reported to their wildlife health line or email. Domestic poultry illness goes to the state agriculture department instead.

For anyone who keeps birds, runs a sanctuary, or lives next to migratory routes, this is the part that lands. Highly pathogenic H5N1 has been circulating in North America since 2022. It did not vanish with the spring the way earlier strains did. It moves with the birds, and it can jump to domestic flocks when wild birds land near pens, ponds, or feed areas. Oregon already confirmed it in a commercial poultry flock in Clackamas County earlier this month. That is the same corridor the waterfowl are using.

We run a sanctuary in rural Tennessee. Our birds are not on the Pacific Flyway, but the principle is the same wherever waterfowl stop. Biosecurity is not a checklist you pull out once a year. It is the fence, the covered feed, the separate footwear for the bird area, the decision not to let free-ranging domestic ducks mix with wild ones when the migrants are thick. One infected bird in the pond can change the math for the whole flock.

The public health side stays low risk for most people, according to the CDC. Since 2024 the United States has seen dozens of human cases, almost all tied to close contact with infected livestock or poultry. Three deaths. The virus is not currently spreading easily person to person. That does not make the animal side optional. A sanctuary that loses its resident ducks or geese to an outbreak is not just dealing with grief and cleanup. It is dealing with quarantine rules, disposal costs, and the long work of rebuilding a flock under tighter restrictions.

Wildlife managers and hunters are watching the same numbers. Ducks Unlimited and state agencies track these pulses because they affect both population estimates and the practical questions of whether managed waterfowl areas stay open. Private fundraising already stepped in this fall in Michigan to keep daily draws running at four state areas after budget cuts. Disease pressure is another variable that can shrink access or force closures.

What moved this week is the volume of reports. Not a single dramatic die-off on camera, but the steady increase that tells you the virus is moving with the birds. ODFW staff are fielding hundreds of calls and emails. They are not asking the public to collect more samples right now. They are asking people to stay out of the way of sick birds and to protect their own flocks.

If you are building systems around animals, this is the reminder that the physical world still sets the rules. An agent can schedule the feed run or flag a camera alert. It cannot replace the decision to keep the wild birds and the sanctuary birds from sharing the same water when the geese are landing by the thousands. Migration season is when the risk concentrates. The people who run the ponds and the pens already know the calendar. This week Oregon just put a public number on it.

Watch the reports. Tighten the perimeter. The birds are moving. So is the virus.
