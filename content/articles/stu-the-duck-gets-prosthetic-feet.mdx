Stu arrived at J and J Farms Animal Sanctuary in Saxonburg, Pennsylvania, in May with no feet. The story that came with him: born missing one leg, got around on the other until winter. Two-legged birds shift weight and tuck a foot into their belly to stay warm. Stu had no spare. Frostbite took the remaining foot. After that he scooted on his belly, using his wings for drive. Calluses formed on the sides of those wings.

The sanctuary posted the intake. Hearts broke. They started looking for dog booties and someone who could build real prosthetics. That someone turned out to be Infinitely Able Pets Orthotics and Prosthetics. Back in May they also took Hank, another duck with profound leg issues, for a consult. Hank got a custom cart. Stu got feet.

Friday the sanctuary posted the photo: STU HAS FEET!!! Monday came the update. He is super excited. He has figured out how to stand up in them. He has managed a few steps. Then he pitches forward. His pectoral muscles are thick from years of wing-powered locomotion. The center of gravity is wrong. He still wants to push with the wings. The legs are new hardware that his body has not learned to trust yet.

CBS Pittsburgh ran the story on the 27th. The photo shows a black-and-white Muscovy standing on blue mats, the prosthetic feet flat and webbed-looking under him. He looks like he is smiling. The sanctuary says they will keep posting progress.

## Why this is the work

Sanctuaries do not get to outsource the hard cases. When a bird shows up missing both feet, the options are euthanasia, permanent indoor management on soft bedding, or engineering a solution. J and J Farms chose the third. They found a shop that builds for pets, drove the ducks there, waited for the fittings, and now they are running the rehab protocol in real time: short sessions, observation, adjustment of the devices, and the long work of retraining a body that has spent months or years compensating with the wrong muscles.

That is the same loop we run here. A bird arrives with a problem the textbooks do not cover. You measure, you try the cheap version, you call the person who has the right tools, you document what happens, and you do not pretend the first fitting is the finish line. Stu standing is the receipt. Stu walking without face-planting is the next ticket.

## What the body already knows

Ducks are built for water and for flight, not for being carried. When the feet are gone the wings become the primary engines for ground travel. The pecs get strong. The balance system recalibrates around a lower, wider base. Putting feet back under him does not instantly restore the old gait. The nervous system still expects the wings to do the work. Every step that ends in a pitch forward is data. The sanctuary is watching the pitch angle, the number of steps before failure, and whether the devices need a different angle or padding.

They already know Hank’s case is harder. Hank has never walked. The cart lets him feel the motion. Toni can push him like an old wooden pull-toy and he loves the speed. Meanwhile they keep using vet wrap, splints, and folded towels to position the remaining good leg. Unconventional methods that appear to be working. One of their other ducks discovered standing and walking on his own the night before an appointment. Six hours of trills. Nobody slept. That is the range of outcomes when you keep the birds alive long enough to try the next thing.

## The quiet part

Most people who see the photo will smile and move on. The sanctuary still has to clean the mats, check the calluses, monitor for pressure sores where the prosthetics meet skin, and decide how much time Stu spends in the devices versus resting. They also have the rest of the flock, the horses, the other special-needs birds, and the ordinary daily feed and water. The prosthetic work is additive. It does not replace the rest of the job.

That is the builder version of animal care. You do not get a clean product launch. You get a working prototype on a living animal that will tell you, immediately and without polite language, whether the design is wrong. Stu’s pitch-forward is the error log. The next revision either reduces the pitch or it does not. There is no demo mode.

J and J Farms has been running this sanctuary long enough to know the difference between a cute post and a durable outcome. They posted the feet. They posted the limitation the next day. They will post the next update when they have one. That is the signal worth reading.

We keep our own birds on solid ground and soft bedding for the same reason. Mobility is not optional. When the standard solution does not exist, you measure the bird in front of you and you build the next best thing. Stu has feet now. The rest is practice.
