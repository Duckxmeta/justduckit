A family of six swans spent part of the weekend in a residential garden in Corby, Northamptonshire, sitting there like they had already ordered the car.

Two adults and four cygnets. First reported near a bowling green on Occupation Road on Saturday. By Sunday they were sunning themselves on Hazelwood Road. Graham Porter and a colleague from The Waterfowl Sanctuary in Godmanchester drove over from Cambridgeshire, rounded them up into big blue Ikea bags, and took them roughly a mile back to water.

Porter later said the birds looked like they were waiting for their taxi. That is not marketing language. That is how a lot of these calls actually feel when you arrive.

## What the call looked like

The Waterfowl Sanctuary is a working rescue and rehab operation for waterfowl. Porter and the team already spend time on the water dealing with fishing line, lost cygnets, and birds that have wandered into places they cannot leave on their own. This one was urban.

They found the family on Hazelwood Road. The adults were calm enough that the volunteers could talk to them while they started wrapping. The cygnets went into the bags. On the drive the younger birds slept. Porter said the poor babies were sleeping as he drove.

At the lake they ran into a local who had already noticed the family was missing and looked relieved when the volunteers showed up with them. That person helped carry all six from the car. Porter cleared brambles and fishing line from the edge before the birds went back in. Then the family swam off together.

They chose Pen Green lakes because reports indicated the nearer boating lake did not normally hold a family of that size. Closest suitable water won.

## Why this is the work

Most people only see the finished version: birds on the water again. The actual job is the phone call, the drive, the bags that happen to be strong enough, and the ten minutes spent clearing line so the same birds do not get hooked the same week.

We run into the same pattern out here. A bird shows up somewhere it should not be. Someone calls. You go. You figure out which water it came from if you can. You move it. You clear the obvious hazards on the way out. Then you go home and wait for the next one.

The Corby family was not injured in the reporting. They had simply walked into a neighborhood. That still required people who already know how to handle adult swans without turning it into a fight, who keep the right bags in the vehicle, and who can read a local lake well enough to put the birds back where they belong instead of dumping them at the first bit of water.

The Waterfowl Sanctuary does this regularly. Rescues, short-term care, and releases. Self-funded. The public-facing part is the occasional photo of birds in the back of a car. The rest is the same unglamorous loop every sanctuary runs: answer the phone, drive, handle the animal, document what you can, and keep the pens ready for the ones that cannot go straight back.

## The part that does not fit in a caption

Porter also took the time at the release site to pull fishing line and cut back brambles. That is not required for the rescue itself. It is the difference between moving a bird and reducing the chance the same problem repeats immediately.

Anyone who keeps waterfowl or runs a small sanctuary already knows the pattern. Lost or wandering birds are common. Fishing line is common. Residential streets next to green space are common. The scarce part is the person who will get in the car, talk to the adults while bagging the cygnets, and still spend the extra minutes clearing the bank.

The BBC piece is short because the outcome was clean. Six birds went home. No one was hurt. The volunteers drove back to Godmanchester. That is the entire story.

It is also the story we actually live. The work is not the dramatic rescue video. It is the ordinary one that ends with the birds swimming off and the bags going back in the truck.
