Stu arrived at J and J Farms Animal Sanctuary in Saxonburg, Pennsylvania, in May with no feet. The history the sanctuary received said he had been born without one leg. He managed on the remaining one until winter. Then frostbite took that foot too.

Two-legged birds shift weight, tuck one leg into belly feathers to warm it, and switch. Stu could not. He scooted. He used his wings for stability and forward motion. Calluses formed on the sides of those wings. The sanctuary posted in May that their hearts were breaking for him.

On October 23 they posted again: “STU HAS FEET!!!” The photo showed him on a blue mat, two new prosthetic feet under him. Infinitely Able Pets Orthotics and Prosthetics built them. A follow-up said he was super excited. He could stand. He managed a few steps before pitching forward.

## The Body Remembers the Old Way

His pectoral muscles are strong from years of wing-powered locomotion. The sanctuary wrote that it will take time for him to adjust his center of gravity and learn to rely on his legs again instead of his wings. That is the real work after the hardware arrives.

We see versions of this at home. A bird that has spent months or years compensating builds a different body. Strength shows up in the wrong places for the new setup. You can hand a duck new feet, but the duck still has to relearn balance, pressure, and what “forward” feels like when the wings are not the primary engine. The first steps are the easy part of the story. The weeks after are the part that decides whether the feet stay useful.

## What the Sanctuary Actually Did

J and J Farms took him in, quarantined him, and started looking for options. They mentioned trying small dog booties and hunting for someone who could make bird prosthetics. Infinitely Able Pets, based in Fairfax, Virginia, does custom orthotics and prosthetics for animals. They evaluated Stu (and another bird named Hank) earlier in the process. The finished pair went on in late October.

Coverage picked up from there. CBS Pittsburgh ran a piece. People and Yahoo carried it. The photos show a black-and-white Muscovy with a red face, standing with both prosthetic feet flat. One image is close enough that you can see the shape of the feet and the black edges on the bottoms. No one claimed he is sprinting across the yard. The update was measured: stand, a few steps, tip forward, keep working.

## Why This Is News for Anyone Who Keeps Birds

Frostbite is not rare on birds that cannot alternate feet or that live in wet, cold conditions without enough shelter. A single-legged duck is already limited. Losing the second foot turns movement into a full-body job. Sanctuaries absorb those cases because most private keepers cannot or will not. The difference here is that a specialist shop built hardware that lets him stand upright again.

That is not a miracle cure. It is a tool. The tool only works if the sanctuary keeps adjusting fit, watching for sores, and giving him time and space to figure out the new mechanics. The same constraints apply at any rescue that takes in birds with missing or damaged feet: you need someone who can fabricate, someone who can monitor daily, and enough room that the bird is not forced to relearn in a crowded pen.

## The Part We Can Use

We run a sanctuary in Tennessee. We do not have a prosthetic shop on speed dial, and we do not pretend every bird that arrives will get custom feet. What we do have is the same problem set: birds that arrive with damage, birds that develop new problems in weather, and the long stretch after the dramatic rescue where the animal has to live with the outcome.

Stu’s case is a concrete example of what “long stretch” looks like. He spent years on one leg, then months scooting on wings. The prosthetics changed the hardware. They did not erase the adaptation. The sanctuary’s own words are the useful ones: center of gravity, pectoral strength, time to relearn. Those are the sentences that matter more than the photo of him standing.

If you keep waterfowl and you see a bird that cannot alternate feet in cold weather, the frostbite risk is real. If you run a rescue and a bird arrives missing feet, the options are limited but not zero. Custom prosthetics exist for birds. They require a specialist, a fitting process, and a long follow-up. The story that travels is the photo. The work that stays is the adjustment period after the photo.

Stu is standing. He is not yet walking the way a two-footed bird walks. The sanctuary said they will keep posting progress. That is the right next report.
