Resident birds are walking back into pens at Broughshane Waterfowl Park. Thirteen of the flock did not make it. More than twenty others spent time at the USPCA after a nearby heating oil tank was vandalised and kerosene reached the pond.

The park in County Antrim had closed. Work on the contamination is still going. Some pens stay closed. The place that held about 120 collection birds has reopened enough for a first group to come home.

## What the oil actually did

Home heating oil is kerosene. It does not just sit on the surface. Birds that swim or feed near the edges pick it up on feathers and feet. Once the waterproofing fails, they lose heat. If they preen, they swallow it. Charcoal and baths help the ones that arrive early enough. Some do not.

The Northern Ireland Environment Agency was called on 22 October. They traced the source to a vandalised tank, put out absorbent booms, and have been removing waste since. The waterway looks better. Oil still clings to edges. Birds that are not native species stay off the pond until the pens are clear.

Gary Hayward, chairman of the Broughshane Environment Wildfowl and Wildlife Trust, said about ten to twelve birds have returned so far. That includes two black swans and Nēnēs. They are back in their pens and healthy enough to be there. He called the USPCA a great help. The park posted thanks to the public and to the people who took the birds in.

Siobhan Hawthorn, wildlife supervisor at the USPCA, said every bird that arrived needed bathing and treatment. Geese, ducks, and swans. Some had already ingested enough that nothing fixed it. Birds hide illness well. The full count of delayed losses is not always obvious on day one.

## Why this is the ordinary hard part of keeping water

Anyone who runs a sanctuary or a collection pond already knows the sequence. Something upstream fails or gets broken. The drainage carries it. You smell it before you see the full extent. Then the day becomes baths, transport, charcoal, and waiting to see who preens again without coating themselves.

The difference here is the source was deliberate vandalism of a heating tank, not a leak that went unnoticed or a farm runoff event. Police asked for information. Local politicians called it reckless. None of that changes the feathers already oiled or the birds already gone.

For the birds that came back, the work is not finished. Pens stay restricted until the remaining oil is gone. Non-native species cannot simply be turned loose into the wider water while residue remains. The park is open again, but open is not the same as normal. Hayward has said it will take weeks before things look like they did.

## What the recovery shows

The first birds back are the ones that survived the transport and the treatment. Two black swans and Nēnēs among the ten or twelve. That is a small number against 120, but it is the number that made it through the acute phase. The USPCA took the load when the park could not keep contaminated birds on site. The Environment Agency did the boom and removal work that volunteers cannot do with buckets.

Public support showed up in the posts. People who visit the park or follow the trust put weight behind the recovery. That matters when a place runs on a trust and volunteers rather than a large staff. The thanks are not abstract. Someone has to drive the birds, someone has to wash them, someone has to keep checking the edges of the pond for the next week or two.

The deaths are not abstract either. Thirteen birds from a collection that people know by sight. Trumpeter swans and ducks were named in earlier updates. A male black swan was reported lost later. Those are the animals that do not return with the first group.

## The part that does not reset

Cleanup removes the visible oil. It does not put the lost birds back or erase the weeks the survivors spent off the pond. For anyone who keeps waterfowl, the lesson is the same one that shows up every time drainage meets a spill: the system has to hold, or the birds take the hit. Vandalism is just a faster version of the same failure.

The birds that walked back into their pens this week are the ones the baths and the charcoal and the transport managed to keep. The rest of the work is the slower part—watching the edges, keeping the restricted pens restricted, and confirming the water is actually clear before more of the flock comes home.
