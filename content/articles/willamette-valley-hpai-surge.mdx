On November 12 the Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife told KLCC that staff had fielded hundreds of calls and emails about sick or dead migratory ducks and geese. Most of the affected birds are Cackling geese, a smaller relative of the Canada goose, and the bulk of the reports are coming out of the Willamette Valley. The same day the Oregon Department of Agriculture confirmed the state’s first positive test for highly pathogenic avian influenza in a commercial dairy herd in Morrow County.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="79" />

## What the numbers actually say

ODFW veterinarian Julia Burco described the clinical picture plainly: infected birds are lethargic, unable to fly, shake their heads, swim in circles, or show cloudy eyes. “They’re pretty non-responsive,” she said. The agency is not asking the public to collect more birds for testing; the volume is already high enough. People who find groups of dead birds are asked to report them by phone at 866-968-2600 or by email to wildlife.health@odfw.oregon.gov. Individual carcasses can be double-bagged after gloving and masking, then placed in the trash. Do not bring sick wild birds to rehabilitators; there is no effective treatment.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="80" />

The dairy case is new. Routine milk surveillance caught the virus on a Morrow County farm. The herd is under quarantine. Oregon has seen the virus in commercial and backyard poultry before; this is the first time it has been confirmed in cattle in the state. Neighboring Washington has logged dozens of dairy positives in recent weeks, so the westward movement was expected by people who watch the maps.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="83" />

## Why a sanctuary operator notices

We run outdoor birds. Migratory waterfowl pass over and around the property every fall. Shared water, shared air, and the occasional visitor bird are normal. A concentrated spike of H5N1 in the same flyway changes the daily calculation. Biosecurity that was previously “good enough for ordinary duck care” becomes the difference between keeping the flock and watching it go down.

The practical moves are the same ones every farm and sanctuary already knows but sometimes postpones: keep domestic birds away from wild water, limit shared equipment, watch for the first neurological signs, and have a disposal plan that does not involve the neighbor’s dog. The dairy confirmation adds a second layer. The virus is not staying inside the bird compartment. Anyone who moves feed, manure, or people between species is now in a larger risk envelope.

## The longer pattern

This is not the 2014–15 outbreak that burned hot and then vanished in spring. The current H5N1 lineage has been circulating in wild birds across North America since 2022 and has shown up in mammals repeatedly. CDC numbers still list the human risk as low—74 U.S. cases and three deaths since 2024, almost all in people with close animal contact. That does not make the operational problem smaller for the person who has to decide whether to lock the ducks down for the rest of migration.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="79" />

Oregon’s open data portal shows a steady accumulation of HPAI confirmations. The Willamette Valley concentration tracks the density of migratory stopovers. Cackling geese flock tightly; once the virus is in a group it moves fast. Ducks can carry it with fewer visible signs, which is why the official advice is not to assume a healthy-looking bird is safe to handle.

## What we are doing differently this week

We are treating every dead wild bird on or near the property as a reportable event. Boots and tools that touch wild water stay outside the main pens. The dairy news means we are also watching the feed and equipment suppliers more closely for any cross-species movement. None of this is dramatic. It is the ordinary work of keeping animals alive when the flyway is running hot.

The concrete development is simple: hundreds of reports of sick and dead migratory waterfowl in the Willamette Valley, plus the first dairy confirmation in Oregon, both landing on November 12. For anyone who keeps outdoor birds or runs a mixed operation, that is the signal. The virus is in the valley right now. The rest is just whether the fences, the water, and the daily routine are tight enough to keep it out of the pens.
