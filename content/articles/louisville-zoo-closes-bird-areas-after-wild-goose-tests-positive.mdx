A wild Canada goose showed up sick on Louisville Zoo grounds. Staff found it, tested it, and the result came back positive for avian influenza. The zoo closed several walk-through bird habitats the same day.

None of the zoo’s own birds are showing clinical signs. The risk to people is still listed as very low by the CDC. Confirmatory testing at a federal lab is pending. The closures are precautionary, expected to last several weeks until the local threat drops.

That is the whole event. One sick wild bird. One positive test. Habitats shut. Biosecurity stepped up.

## What Actually Closed

Public walk-through aviaries went dark. Forest Bird Trail and the African penguin exhibits in the Islands Pavilion. Steller’s Sea Eagle Aviary in Glacier Run. Lorikeet Landing in Australia. Some areas around the zoo lake. Keepers who work the bird areas started disinfecting footwear on the way in and tightened daily health checks.

Zoo director Dan Maloney said the virus has been in the region for a few years and the teams are trained for it. Temporary closures cut risk to the collection and keep visitors safe. The zoo itself stayed open.

Wild waterfowl and shorebirds carry these viruses. Migration season moves them. A sick bird can land on the grounds, and the next step is containment before it has a chance to touch the birds that live there year-round.

## Why This Lands Here

We keep waterfowl. The birds we care for do not live behind zoo fencing or under daily veterinary staff. They live outside, with wild birds flying over the property, landing in the ponds, and walking the same grass. Highly pathogenic avian influenza is not theoretical for anyone who runs birds outdoors. It is a seasonal pressure that shows up when the migration is on.

A zoo with professional staff, controlled access, and the ability to close public areas still treats a single positive wild goose as enough reason to lock down the aviaries. That is the baseline response when the infrastructure exists. On a sanctuary or a small farm, the same virus arrives with fewer levers. You cannot close the whole property to the public if the public is not the main risk. The main risk is the birds already there and the wild ones that keep coming.

The practical pieces that move are the ones the zoo listed: footwear disinfection, heightened daily observation, and limiting contact between wild birds and the collection. Those are not new protocols. They are the ones that get enforced when a positive test shows up nearby.

## The Cost of Waiting for Symptoms

The zoo’s own birds were still healthy when the goose tested positive. That is the window. Once clinical signs appear in the collection, the response is no longer just habitat closure. It becomes testing, isolation, and, in bad cases, depopulation. The early move is to treat the wild bird as the signal and reduce the pathways before the collection shows anything.

For operations that do not have a full veterinary team on site, the signal is thinner. You notice a wild bird that looks off, or you hear about positives in the area, or you see your own birds acting quieter than usual. The decision is the same: tighten the controls you already have or expand them. Extra disinfection. Fewer shared water sources if you can manage it. Watching the flock harder for a stretch of weeks.

None of that is a guarantee. HPAI has moved through commercial poultry and backyard flocks in waves. Sanctuaries sit in the same landscape. The difference is scale and the ability to notice the first bird before the second and third show signs.

## What Stays Open

The zoo stayed open to the public. Only the bird walk-throughs closed. That split is useful. The rest of the facility kept running while the highest-risk contact points went dark. Visitors were told not to feed wild birds. The message is straightforward: the virus is here, the collection is still clear, and the response is limited to the places where transmission is easiest.

For a sanctuary the equivalent is keeping the core work going while raising the floor on biosecurity for a defined period. The birds still need food, clean water, and observation. The extra steps sit on top of that, not instead of it. The goal is the same one the zoo named: protect the animals that live there while the wild birds keep moving through the area.

The goose was found sick on the grounds. That is the concrete fact. Everything after it is the response that follows when the test is positive and the collection has not yet shown signs. For anyone who keeps birds outside, that sequence is the one worth watching. The virus does not wait for perfect infrastructure. It arrives with the next wild bird that lands.

The Louisville Zoo did the early version of the protocol. Closed the walk-throughs. Stepped up disinfection. Watched the collection. The confirmatory lab result is still out. Until the threat drops, the habitats stay shut. That is the current state of play.
