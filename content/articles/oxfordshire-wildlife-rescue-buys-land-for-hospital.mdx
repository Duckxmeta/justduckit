Oxfordshire Wildlife Rescue closed on land in Sutton Courtenay this week. The charity will build a dedicated wildlife hospital there after years of operating out of a rented casualty centre that had already hit its limit.

Luke Waclawek, founder and CEO, said he made a promise in 2020, during lockdown, that Oxfordshire would one day have its own dedicated wildlife hospital. On 21 October the organisation announced it had become the owner of land south of Hobbyhorse Lane in Sutton Courtenay. Building has not started. No opening date is set. The aim is to begin work by May 2027.

## The capacity problem that forced the move

The current centre sits in Blewbury. It takes thousands of animals a year. In late September the team closed to new admissions because of a temporary staff shortage and full capacity. The ambulance stayed available for large-mammal emergencies on a limited basis. That is the practical ceiling of a rented site that started as a family home and outgrew itself.

Most of the animals that arrive are the result of something people did. The charity’s own note is blunt: 97 percent of cases trace back to human activity, direct or indirect. Hedgehogs alone hit a record 153 patients in a single month this August. Swans, waterfowl, foxes, deer, badgers, and the rest of the local list keep coming. A purpose-built hospital is the difference between turning animals away and having the rooms, aviaries, and triage bays to take them.

## What the new site is supposed to hold

The plans call for on-site triage and veterinary facilities, specialist patient wards, a nursery and early-care space for young animals, aviaries, waterfowl areas, and mammal rehabilitation spaces. Dedicated hedgehog care is called out specifically because the volume is already there. The site is also intended as a training ground for veterinary students. Waclawek has said that building the next generation of people who can treat wildlife is as important as the daily work.

Supporters raised over £100,000 toward the appeal. Individual stakeholders helped close the gap. The land itself was the hard part. The charity spent nearly two years fighting for it after receiving notice to leave the Blewbury rental. Earlier fundraising targets sat around £250,000 for a permanent home. Owning the dirt removes the annual uncertainty that comes with a landlord’s timeline.

## Why the dirt matters more than the building

A sanctuary operator already knows the pattern. You start with the animals that arrive. You patch the space you have. You hit the wall of beds, water systems, isolation rooms, and hands. Then you either stop taking new cases or you buy the next piece of ground so the work can continue. Oxfordshire is doing the second thing while the first is still running.

The waterfowl areas matter here. Migratory and resident birds show up injured, oiled, or orphaned on the same calendar that floods and freezes the rest of the region. Having dedicated space for them instead of sharing pens with everything else is the operational difference between “we can take this one” and “we are full.” Same logic applies to the hedgehog wards. Volume is not theoretical when the monthly count is already in the hundreds.

The timeline is still open. No groundbreaking has happened. The May 2027 start date is a target, not a guarantee. The charity remains closed to new non-emergency admissions until capacity and staffing catch up. That gap is the part that does not wait for the new building.

## What a builder who also runs a sanctuary sees

The concrete event is the land purchase. Everything else is the backlog that made the purchase necessary. We see the same constraint on our own ground: the physical limit arrives before the mission ends. You can write the protocols, train the volunteers, and post the success stories. None of it expands the square footage or the isolation bays. Buying the site is the step that converts the promise into a place the animals can actually occupy.

Oxfordshire still has to raise the rest of the capital, design the buildings, and staff the hospital once it opens. The land removes the single largest uncertainty. The rest is the ordinary work of turning a field into a facility that can take the next swan, the next hedgehog, and the next orphaned waterfowl without turning them away.
