Oxfordshire Wildlife Rescue owns land now.

Luke Waclawek, founder and CEO, said it plain: after nearly two years of fighting for the ground, the charity is the owner of a site in Sutton Courtenay. Supporters put up over £100,000 toward the appeal. Some individual stakeholders stepped in as well. Building has not started. No opening date is set. They aim to break ground by May 2027.

That is the move that matters.

## The place they outgrew

They began in Luke and Ruth’s house in 2018. The casualty centre in Blewbury grew from there. It is the first call for a lot of people who find a sick, injured, or orphaned wild animal in Oxfordshire and the surrounding counties. They run a 24-hour wildlife ambulance. Staff and volunteers cover more than 200 species — deer, foxes, badgers, mice, swans, birds of prey, and the rest.

The Blewbury site is no longer big enough. By the end of September it was at full capacity and closed to new admissions. The animals already inside still need care. The next call still comes.

August made the pressure obvious. One hundred and fifty-three hedgehogs arrived sick or injured. That was a record for a single animal group in one month. The new hospital will have dedicated hedgehog care. It will also have on-site triage and vet facilities, specialist patient wards, a nursery for young animals, aviaries, waterfowl areas, and mammal rehabilitation spaces.

## What the new site actually is

Land south of Hobbyhorse Lane. Purpose-built instead of adapted. The plan includes training for vet and animal-care students. Waclawek called that part “just as important as the work we do today.” Students build experience; the wildlife gets more hands that already know the routine.

The charity is still at the planning stage. It will be the first of its kind in the county. They are looking for construction partners, people who can sponsor an aviary or a ward, volunteers for work parties, and anyone who can help with trades or admin. The hospital is for the community and the local wildlife. The invitation is open.

## Why the land changes the math

A rescue that rents its ground lives with a clock. A notice to leave can arrive. Every expansion has to fit someone else’s timeline and someone else’s rules. Owning the site removes that particular risk. The work can be designed around the animals that actually arrive instead of around the rooms that already exist.

We know the version of this that lives in the pen layout and the feed room. When the next animal shows up and the current setup is already full, the mission does not pause. You make space, or you make a hard call, or both. Physical capacity is not a branding problem. It is the number of stalls, the number of runs, the square footage that can hold a recovery without stacking animals on top of each other.

Oxfordshire just bought the first piece of that. The rest is still ahead of them — permits, construction, staffing the larger place, keeping the ambulance running while the new rooms go up. The £100,000 is a start, not the whole bill. The land is the part that lets the rest of the plan exist.

## What we take from it

A sanctuary that outgrows its current footprint has two options that matter: keep turning animals away, or secure ground and build. They chose the second. The details that still need checking are the exact size of the parcel, the final construction budget, and how soon planning clears. The fact that moved is the ownership.

If you run animals, or you build tools that people who run animals use, this is the part of the work that does not fit in a dashboard. The land has to be there before the better software or the better feed system can scale with the call volume. They now have the land. The hospital is the next concrete thing.
