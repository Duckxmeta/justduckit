On November 19 Google announced a $750,000 grant to Ducks Unlimited for Ducks Unlimited Park in West Memphis, Arkansas. It is the first check from a $10 million commitment to community water sources, infrastructure, and access across Arkansas. The money is earmarked for a pavilion, walking and biking trails, outdoor classrooms, and bathrooms at a 1,500-acre floodplain site across the river from downtown Memphis.

The park already has a paved seven-mile loop and a half-mile out-and-back trail. People walk, run, and ride it for the views of the Mississippi and the Memphis skyline. Ducks Unlimited plans to restore native vegetation, recreate functional wetlands, and turn former farmland into bottomland hardwood and prairie that can hold and filter water. Once the work is done the property is expected to transfer to the City of West Memphis and Big River Park Conservancy for long-term management.

Google’s stated reason is its data-center footprint. The company is building a large campus in West Memphis and has a goal of replenishing more water than it consumes by 2030. Data centers need water for cooling. The grant is framed as part of managing that use in the places where the buildings go up. Ben Townsend, Google’s Global Head of Infrastructure Strategy & Sustainability, said water is vital for ecosystems and communities and that the company has both an opportunity and a responsibility to protect it where it operates.

Dr. Karen Waldrop, Ducks Unlimited’s Chief Operating Officer, called the park a place where conservation and community work together, restoring floodplain habitat while giving people a place to be outside. That is the concrete thing that happened. A tech company writing a check for wetland work next to its own industrial site.

## Why the numbers matter on the ground

Fifteen hundred acres of floodplain is not a backyard pond. When the river rises the land takes water. Restored wetlands and forests slow that water, drop sediment, and can cut the load of nitrogen and other pollutants that move downstream. Similar restored sites in the Mississippi Alluvial Valley have been measured at sequestering several tons of carbon dioxide equivalent per acre per year and reducing nitrogen by tens of kilograms per acre. The park is not a closed system; it is part of the working river. Trails and a pavilion make the restoration visible to people who do not own boats or duck blinds.

For a builder who also runs animals, the interesting part is the adjacency. Google is not donating to a distant sanctuary. It is putting money into habitat that sits next to the physical plant that will run the models and services. Water is an input cost and an operating constraint. Habitat is the buffer that keeps the river usable for everyone else. The two are not separate ledgers once you are actually building something that pulls power and water.

We see the same tension at smaller scale. A sanctuary needs reliable water, drainage that does not fail in a hard rain, and land that is not already spoken for by someone else’s expansion. The difference is the size of the check and the size of the footprint. Google’s $750,000 is a fraction of the $10 million it has committed in Arkansas and a smaller fraction of the capital going into the data center itself. It is still enough to finish amenities that let the public use the site while the longer restoration work continues.

## What is still missing

Google has not said how the remaining $9.25 million will be spent. The park grant is the first named piece. The broader pledge is tied to water stewardship around data centers, but the exact projects, timelines, and measurable outcomes for the rest of the money are not public. Restoration numbers for pollutant reduction and water retention at this specific site are still qualitative. The park is open now; the full wetland conversion is multi-year work.

Earlier funding already exists. In 2024 the U.S. Department of Transportation awarded roughly $16.2 million through the PROTECT program for floodplain restoration at the same park. Other private partners have put money in over the past few years. Google’s grant sits on top of that stack rather than replacing it. The amenities it funds (pavilion, trails, classrooms) are the pieces that turn a restoration project into a place people actually visit.

Volunteers from Google, Ducks Unlimited, Big River Park Conservancy, and other local groups already cleaned litter at the park around the announcement. The ground is real. The check is real. The longer test is whether the restoration holds water the way the models and the permits claim it will, and whether the rest of the $10 million shows up as additional habitat or just more infrastructure language.

## The closer for anyone who builds and keeps animals

When a company the size of Google decides the water next to its data center is worth writing a check for wetlands, that is a data point. It does not solve the larger problem of how much water large compute facilities actually use, or how the rest of the pledge gets allocated. It does show that the physical site and the living systems around it are now part of the same conversation the company is having with local governments and conservation groups.

For the rest of us the lesson is smaller and more practical. Habitat work that is visible and usable gets funded more easily than habitat work that is only measured in tons of carbon or gallons filtered. Trails and a classroom make the restoration legible. If you are trying to keep land open for animals while other people build around you, the pieces that let neighbors walk the edge of the property tend to travel better than the pure biology. The biology still has to work. The check just arrived for the part people can see.
