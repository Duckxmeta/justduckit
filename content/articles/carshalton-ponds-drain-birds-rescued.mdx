The ponds in Carshalton went dry again. Last week they were completely empty. After rain they still held only mud and a few centimetres of water. Birds had to be rescued. A volunteer from The Swan Sanctuary said the conditions were dangerous.

Sutton Council emptied the upper pond in July to fix a leak. They expected the water company to refill it. Drought rules stopped the pumps. The drop that followed was faster and deeper than drought alone explains. Specialists are now looking for the real problem.

## What the water did

Carshalton ponds sit in a well-known local spot and feed the River Wandle. Natural springs run through pipes into the concrete-lined basins built in 1968. When the springs are low, SES Water normally pumps water in. This summer the Environment Agency kept south London in drought status, one level below the most serious category. The pumps stayed off.

Councillor Jake Short noted that the topping-up stops in dry spells. Strategic director Spencer Palmer said the drought does not account for how far the levels fell. The council is investigating what else is broken.

Vuki Popovic of The Swan Sanctuary described the risk plainly. Low, stagnant water can produce toxins. Birds get stranded. Swans cannot take off when the water is too shallow. Juliet Rains, who feeds the ducks there, noticed the drop from the road. The usual high water was gone.

## Why a sanctuary operator cares

We run birds on managed water. We know what happens when the level moves faster than the animals can. Domestic ducks cannot fly away from a bad pond the way wild ones can. Even wild waterfowl on a public pond still need enough depth to escape predators, to preen, and to leave. Mud flats and a few centimetres leave them exposed. Rescue becomes the default instead of habitat.

The same pattern shows up anywhere people rely on a pipe or a pump to keep a wetland looking alive. One leak, one drought restriction, one unmonitored valve, and the system fails. The animals do not get a second chance while specialists are called. That is the operational fact. Habitat is infrastructure. When the infrastructure is concrete and pipes instead of living wetland, the failure modes are sudden and total.

## The second failure this year

This is not the first time. The upper pond was drained on purpose in July for edge repairs near Honeywood Museum. More issues appeared while the work was underway. Water kept leaving. By mid-October the overnight drop was dramatic enough that residents posted photos of bare mud. The council has been monitoring and has now brought in outside specialists. No long-term fix is announced.

Public ponds like these are often treated as scenery. They are also working habitat for ducks, swans, and everything that uses the water. When the water leaves, the rescue load lands on local volunteers and wildlife groups. The Swan Sanctuary is already doing that work. Most small sanctuaries do not have a spare crew waiting for the next municipal pond to empty.

## What builders and operators can take from it

If you keep birds, the lesson is simple. Know your water source and its failure modes. A spring-fed pond with a concrete liner and a seasonal pump is not the same as a natural wetland that can shrink and expand. Document the normal range. Watch the rate of change, not just the absolute level. Have a plan for temporary holding that does not rely on the same failing system.

If you build or maintain public or private water features, the same rule applies. Habitat is not a side effect of the landscaping. It is the function. When the function stops, the animals still have to go somewhere. The specialists will arrive. The birds cannot wait.

Carshalton is one set of ponds in south London. The pattern is not unique. Water management that treats living habitat as an afterthought will keep producing these rescues. The concrete and the pipes do not care. The birds do.
