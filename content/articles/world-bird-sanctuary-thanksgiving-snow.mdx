Wet, heavy snow does not care that you built the roof to let it pass through. On Thanksgiving night it stuck to the netting at World Bird Sanctuary in Valley Park, Missouri, loaded the cables and posts, and took down twelve large aviaries. Three of them had only been finished since August. Executive Director Roger Holloway called it the worst weather damage he has seen in thirty-eight years at the place.

None of the birds were hurt. Several flew into nearby trees when the structures failed. By early afternoon staff still had three unaccounted for, though they stayed close enough that trainers could keep eyes on them. The sanctuary closed indefinitely. The public side is down. The rehab enclosures that hold wild birds in treatment were untouched.

That is the concrete thing that happened. Here is why it matters if you also keep animals and try to build anything that lasts.

## The design that met the wrong snow

The aviaries are open-topped on purpose. Most snow and leaves are supposed to fall through. That works for ordinary winter weather. This system brought the wet kind that clings. Once it loaded the mesh, the weight transferred to the side posts and the support cables. Holloway described the result as catastrophic: netting torn, cables stressed, posts damaged. Half of the twelve will probably need to be rebuilt from scratch rather than patched.

We have watched the same physics on smaller scale with run covers and shade cloth. Light snow sheds. Sticky snow turns a permeable roof into a solid load. You can design for the average event and still get cleaned out by the one that sticks. The difference at World Bird Sanctuary is the scale: twelve large enclosures, three of them brand new, on a 300-plus-acre property that already houses more than two hundred animals across sixty species.

The permanent residents live in those public aviaries. Some are injured and cannot be released. Others were raised around people and are unreleasable. They are not temporary patients. When the structure fails, those birds lose the space they have known for years. Staff spent the holiday pulling them back out of trees and into temporary holding. That work is straightforward and exhausting at the same time.

## Money, insurance, and the gap

Insurance will cover some of the repair. Holloway was clear that it will not cover all of it. Donations are open at the sanctuary’s Qgiv page. The organization has been running since 1977. It is a working raptor hospital and rehabilitation center next to Lone Elk County Park, not a seasonal exhibit. Closing the public side means lost visits, lost education programs, and the daily cost of feeding and caring for the residents while the rebuild happens.

Months is the timeline they are using. Dismantle the wreckage, order materials, rebuild under whatever winter still has left, then restock. Meanwhile the birds need somewhere to live. Temporary setups have their own feed bills, labor hours, and failure modes. Anyone who has moved a flock into backup housing knows the second set of problems starts the moment the first set is solved.

We keep thinking about the three new aviaries finished in August. Fresh infrastructure, already gone. That is the part that hits a builder. You finish the thing, you put animals in it, and a single weather event writes the whole capital project off. The lesson is not “build stronger.” The lesson is that the contingency fund and the insurance conversation have to be as real as the lumber order.

## What the rest of the region looked like

The same storm dropped two to seven and a half inches across the St. Louis metro, with higher totals farther south. More than a hundred thousand customers lost power. An ice arena in Wentzville collapsed. Tree limbs came down across the area. World Bird Sanctuary was not the only structure that met wet snow it was not sized for. It was simply the one whose job is keeping birds alive.

Staff who were off for the holiday came in. Trainers worked the trees. The update at 2 p.m. still listed three birds outside their enclosures. By evening the public statements were the same: closed until further notice, donations needed, birds unharmed. That is the operational reality. The photos show collapsed wooden frames and snow-loaded netting sagging between the posts. No dramatic injuries. Just the quiet math of a facility that has to keep functioning while half its outdoor housing is unusable.

## For anyone who runs the physical side

If you keep animals, weather is not a content topic. It is a line item that can erase a year of capital work in one night. The permeable roof that works most of the time becomes the failure mode when the snow is wet. The new build that just passed inspection is the one that has not yet been tested by the event it was not designed for. The insurance check that arrives later does not feed the birds this week.

World Bird Sanctuary has the institutional memory of thirty-eight years and still got hit harder than anything in that record. The response was the practical one: get the birds in, assess the structures, open the donation link, tell the public the place is closed. That is the version of the work that does not make for clean before-and-after photos.

We run our own birds on different ground, with different species and a much smaller footprint. The physics of a loaded roof is the same. So is the gap between what insurance covers and what the animals still need the next morning. The sanctuary in Valley Park just showed the scale of that gap in public. The rebuild will take months. The birds are still there every day in between.

If you can send something toward the repair, the link is on their site. If you cannot, the concrete takeaway is still useful: design for the snow that sticks, keep the contingency real, and treat the first night after the failure as the actual test of the operation.
