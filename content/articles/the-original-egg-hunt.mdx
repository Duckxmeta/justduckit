When spring hits, the world feels like it’s waking up from a long nap. For most people, Easter is marked by plastic grass and store-bought chocolate. But for those of us with boots on the ground (and likely covered in a little mud), Easter feels a lot more literal.

Between the blooming flora and the sudden explosion of eggs in the coop, the holiday serves as a bridge between ancient tradition and the daily reality of raising waterfowl.

### 1. The Significance of Easter: Renewal and Rebirth

At its core, Easter is a celebration of renewal. While it holds deep religious significance, its timing is no accident. It coincides with the spring equinox — the moment the Earth tilts back toward the sun and life returns to dormant fields.

Historically, this was a time of survival. After a long winter of lean food supplies, the return of the sun meant fresh greens, the birth of livestock, and the return of the egg.

### 2. The Origins of the Idea: Why Eggs?

Why do we hide brightly colored shells in the grass? The tradition is a beautiful mashup of history:

- **The Universe in a Shell**: Ancient Egyptians, Persians, and Romans viewed the egg as a symbol of the entire universe — the seed of life that held the potential for everything.
- **The Forbidden Food**: During the Middle Ages, eggs were forbidden during the 40-day fast of Lent. Since the birds kept laying, farmers hard-boiled the eggs to preserve them.
- **The “Special” Batch**: To distinguish the older Lent eggs from fresh ones, people began staining them with vegetables, roots, and flowers. By Easter Sunday, the decorated “Easter Egg” was born.

### 3. The Natural Egg Hunt: Life with Ducks

If you’ve ever owned ducks, you know that every morning is an Easter egg hunt. Unlike chickens, who generally use nesting boxes, ducks are creative with their delivery.

You’ll find them:

- Buried six inches deep in a pile of fresh straw
- Hidden in a fortress of tall grass by the fence line
- Submerged at the bottom of a muddy puddle

There is a primitive, satisfying joy in scanning a yard and spotting that smooth, matte curve of a freshly laid egg. It’s a literal gift from the flock signaling that the season of abundance has arrived.

### 4. Nature’s Pastel Palette: No Dye Required

We spend hours with vinegar and food coloring trying to mimic what ducks do naturally. Depending on the breed, duck eggs come in a stunning range of colors:

- **Classic White**: Common in Pekins — bright, clean, and almost marble-like.
- **Seafoam Blue & Mint Green**: Often seen in Runners and Magpies.
- **Cream & Apricot**: Warm, buttery tones from certain breeds.
- **Charcoal / “Soot”**: Cayugas can lay nearly jet-black or deep charcoal eggs at the start of the season that gradually fade to grey and then white.

Whether you’re celebrating the religious tradition or simply the fact that your yard is finally green again, the duck egg remains one of the ultimate symbols of the season — a reminder that life is persistent, colorful, and, if you’re lucky, waiting for you in a nest of straw tomorrow morning.
