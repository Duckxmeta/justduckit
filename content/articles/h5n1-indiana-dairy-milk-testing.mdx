On November 2 the USDA’s National Veterinary Services Laboratories confirmed H5N1 genotype B3.13 in a dairy herd in Indiana. The signal came from routine bulk-tank testing under the National Milk Testing Strategy, not from a farmer calling about sick cows or from a pre-movement check. Indiana is the 21st state with confirmed H5N1 in dairy cattle since the outbreak began in March 2024.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="77" />

Two operationally connected herds in Newton County, in the northwest corner of the state, tested positive. Indiana lost its “unaffected” status. Lactating cows on the affected premises are quarantined. Farms and commercial poultry operations inside a 10-kilometer zone face extra surveillance. Any lactating dairy cow leaving Indiana now needs a negative H5N1 test before it crosses state lines.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="79" />

The same week, New York’s Department of Agriculture reminded dairy and poultry producers to tighten biosecurity after detections in Washington and Indiana. No cases have been found in New York livestock yet, but the department is watching migratory birds and keeping its own testing rules in place.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="78" />

## What the milk test actually found

The National Milk Testing Strategy samples bulk raw milk on an ongoing basis. A positive result in a processing plant outside Indiana led state officials to sample the source herds. Federal labs confirmed the genotype. B3.13 is the lineage that has driven most of the U.S. dairy cattle outbreak since the original spillover from wild birds in the Texas Panhandle.

This is different from the way many earlier detections arrived. Some herds were found because animals looked sick. Others were caught during mandatory testing before cattle moved between states. Indiana’s case was caught by the milk program before clinical signs drove the discovery. That does not mean the virus is milder. It means the surveillance net is wider than it was two years ago.

USDA says the commercial milk supply remains safe. Pasteurization inactivates the virus. Milk from affected animals is diverted or destroyed. Only milk from healthy animals enters processing for people. The CDC still rates the risk to the general public as low. Workers who handle infected birds, cattle, or other animals face higher risk and are told to use the recommended precautions.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="77" />

## The poultry side is still moving

HPAI continues in commercial poultry and backyard flocks while wild birds migrate south. Iowa confirmed H5N1 in a commercial turkey flock in Sac County on October 28. That was Iowa’s eighth detection of 2026. The governor issued a disaster proclamation for the county through November 27 so the state could move resources for monitoring, disposal, and disinfection. In the prior 30 days, poultry cases were also reported in California, Illinois, Minnesota, Wisconsin, South Dakota, North Dakota, and Kansas, among others.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="76" />

Wild birds can carry the virus without looking sick. Domestic poultry often die. Sanctuaries, backyard flocks, and small farms sit in the same landscape as the commercial operations and the migratory pathways. A sanctuary that keeps waterfowl or mixed livestock has to treat every incoming wild bird or every new placement as a potential contact risk. Biosecurity is not a poster on the gate. It is daily habits around feed, water, clothing, and vehicle movement.

## Why this lands on a sanctuary

We run animals. We also watch the same migratory routes and the same weather systems that move wild waterfowl. When H5N1 expands its footprint in dairy cattle, the practical questions for anyone keeping birds or mixed species are immediate: How close are the nearest commercial poultry or dairy operations? What is the current testing and movement rule for the state? Are the feed deliveries and the people who visit the property tracking their own contacts with livestock?

The Indiana detection does not change the core advice that has been in place since 2024. Report unusual illness or death in livestock or wildlife to the state veterinarian. Keep poultry and other birds separated from wild waterfowl as much as the site allows. Limit visitor access to animal areas. Keep records of animal movements and of people who work with multiple species. The milk-testing result simply shows that the virus can be present before the animals look sick enough for a producer to notice.

A builder who also runs a sanctuary has two clocks. One is the code and the deployment schedule. The other is the feed store, the weather, and the health of the birds in the pens. Both clocks keep running. When a new state is added to the dairy list and poultry cases continue during migration, the second clock gets louder for a few days. The response is the same one that has worked on the ground for years: tighten the habits that keep virus off the property, keep good records, and talk to the state animal-health people before you need them.

The commercial milk supply is still safe. The risk to people who do not work with infected animals remains low. The risk to the birds and the other animals on a sanctuary is the one that has to be managed every morning.
