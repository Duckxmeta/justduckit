Oregon wildlife officials are seeing more sick and dead waterfowl in the Willamette Valley than they want. The birds are mostly Cackling geese, a smaller relative of the Canada goose, and the cause is highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1. The reports started building in early November and were still climbing when local stations picked them up again on November 18. This is not a backyard care tip. It is a live movement of virus down the Pacific Flyway that any place holding ducks has to treat as a boundary condition.

### What ODFW is actually counting

The Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife put out a clear press note on November 9. Many geese migrating through the state are showing clinical signs. Most of the calls and emails are coming from the Willamette Valley. Cackling geese are taking the heaviest visible hit. Typical signs include lethargy, inability to fly, swimming in circles, head shaking, cloudy eyes, and loss of coordination. Birds that show those signs usually die within 72 hours. Some dabbling ducks can carry and shed the virus in feces without looking sick at all.

State wildlife veterinarian Dr. Colin Gillin told NBC16 the same week that crews were seeing a lot of deceased or sick Canada-type geese. The virus is showing up especially near dams and reservoirs where birds congregate. Cascade Raptor Center in Eugene has already lost two raptors that came into their hospital last month. Chintimini Wildlife Center in Corvallis closed waterbird intakes and locked down quarantine protocols. That is the practical shape of the outbreak right now: more carcasses on the ground, tighter rules at the places that try to help wildlife, and no treatment available once a bird is infected.

### Why this matters to a sanctuary that keeps ducks

We run Decent Ducks Sanctuary in Middle Tennessee. Our birds are domestic. They do not migrate. They still live outdoors, near water, and next to wild birds that do. H5N1 does not care about the property line. It moves in feces, in water, and on shoes or tires. ODFW is explicit that sick wild birds cannot go to rehabilitation centers because the virus spreads too fast and there is no effective treatment. The same logic applies to a private sanctuary. Once it is inside the fence, you are managing a die-off, not a rescue.

The human risk remains low according to the CDC. Since 2024 the United States has recorded 74 confirmed human cases and three deaths. One of those deaths was reported in Washington state this month in a person who had contact with infected dairy cattle. The virus has already jumped into dairy herds and commercial poultry elsewhere. That does not change the day-to-day fact for people who keep waterfowl: the highest-probability path onto our place is a wild bird landing in the pond or a visitor tracking contaminated mud past the gate.

### The operational layer, not the lecture

ODFW is telling the public to avoid contact with sick or dead birds, keep pets away, and if a carcass has to be moved, use gloves, a mask, and double-bag it for the trash. They do not want people catching live sick birds and driving them to a rehabber. Domestic flock owners are supposed to call the Oregon Department of Agriculture if their own birds go down. None of that is new advice. What is new is the density of the reports in one valley during peak migration.

For us the practical translation is simple. Lock down visitor traffic near the birds when wild waterfowl are on the move. Keep feed and water stations from becoming shared platforms. Watch the pond edges. Have a plan for isolation that does not rely on a wildlife rehab center accepting the bird. The same biosecurity steps that protect commercial poultry are the ones that protect a sanctuary flock; the difference is we do not have the option of depopulating and starting over.

### The longer pattern

This strain has been circulating in North America since 2022. It did not disappear after the first spring. Fall migration is when the numbers spike because the birds are moving and packing together. Oregon is not unique. Similar signals have shown up in other flyways. The Willamette reports just happen to be the loudest local story in the last ten days, and they are specific enough to act on.

If you keep ducks, geese, or any waterfowl, the question is not whether the virus exists somewhere. It is whether your fence, your water, and your visitor habits are treating this particular fall as higher-risk than last year. The Oregon numbers say the risk is higher right now on that flyway. The rest of us still share the same air and the same migratory routes.
