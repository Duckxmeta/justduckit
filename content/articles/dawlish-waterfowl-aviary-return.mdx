Dawlish Town Council asked its people what to do with the empty aviary. Ninety-six percent said put the waterfowl back.

A year ago the Animal and Plant Health Agency ordered a cull. Up to 50 ornamental birds—tufted duck, mandarin, shelduck, and the rest—were humanely destroyed after H5N1 showed up in the compound. Two black swans on the open water died the same week. The free-roaming ones were spared and watched. The aviary sat empty because reopening sooner would have meant expensive, repeated disinfection. That window has closed. The site can come back. The town just had to decide if it wanted the birds again.

They did.

## The vote is the headline. The rebuild is the work.

The Civic Amenities Committee has the results. Temporary fencing is up. Crews are already clearing the old wooden and netted structure. Surveyors and architects are being brought in. The brief is practical: better housing for the birds, better facilities for the people who will care for them, something that sits right in the conservation area, and welfare and environmental rules that actually hold up when the next flu season arrives.

The council is also looking at UK waterfowl breeds and talking to people who know the birds. No overnight restocking. Regular updates, because an empty aviary and radio silence frustrates people who used to walk past the birds on the way to the shops.

That is the part that matters to anyone who has ever signed a feed invoice. A consultation percentage does not keep a bird dry when the roof leaks. It does not keep wild birds off the water when the next housing order drops. England is under a national mandatory housing order for captive birds right now—anything over 50 birds, or anyone who sells eggs or birds, has to house them. Biosecurity rules sit on top of that. The Dawlish site is being redesigned under that pressure, not in a vacuum.

## What the numbers do not tell you

Forty to fifty birds is a small flock compared with a commercial house. It is still enough animals that one sick bird becomes a logistics problem. The cull was not optional. The virus was confirmed. The rules were clear. The emotional hit was real—the black swans and the aviary birds were a local symbol. Twelve months later the town still wants the symbol back, but the people who will actually run the place are talking about improved housing and warden facilities first.

That sequence is correct. You do not restock a site that just had a mandatory cull without changing the physical setup. You change the housing, the access for the people doing the daily work, the way wild birds can reach the water, and the cleaning routine. Then you pick the birds. The council is doing the order right.

Out here the same math shows up at smaller scale. A handful of ducks on a Tennessee place still need dry shelter when the weather turns, a way to separate sick birds fast, and feed that does not run out because someone liked the idea of ducks more than the work. The percentage on a survey does not change the morning check.

## Why a builder who runs a sanctuary watches this

We watch because the hard part is never the vote. The hard part is the second and third year, when the new fencing is in, the new birds are settled, and the next wave of wild-bird detections shows up on the national map. The housing order already exists. The rules will tighten or loosen based on what the virus does next. A town aviary is not a commercial farm, but it sits in the same regulatory weather. The people who get the rebuild right will have an enclosure that can be cleaned, a place for the wardens to work without climbing over temporary netting, and a plan for which breeds can handle the conditions they actually have.

The ones who treat the 96 percent as the finish line will be cleaning up again in two years.

That is the difference between a story that ends at the consultation and a place that still has birds in it. Dawlish is choosing the second path. The work starts now, while the old structure is coming down and the architects are still drawing. That is when the decisions that matter get locked in.

The birds will come back if the housing does the job. The percentage already said yes. The rest is concrete, fencing, and the daily routine that keeps the next cull from happening.
