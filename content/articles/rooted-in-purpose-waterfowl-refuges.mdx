On November 6 the Department of the Interior and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service put out Rooted in Purpose, the results of a six-month review of the National Wildlife Refuge System, plus a Secretary’s Order telling the Service to act on it.

The order and the 29 recommendations are straightforward: put more people and money on the ground, keep the practical tools for habitat work, cut layers that slow the work down, and remember why most of these places exist in the first place.

A lot of them exist for waterfowl.

### What the paper actually says

The review ran from mid-December 2025 through mid-May 2026 under Director Brian Nesvik. Reviewers hit 41 refuges in person, another 118 virtually, plus regional offices. They ran two internal surveys that each pulled more than a thousand responses and an external one that pulled more than two thousand. They talked to states, tribes, private landowners, hunting and conservation groups, and the people who actually manage the land.

The output is not a new vision statement. It is a course correction. Habitat has to be actively managed. Prescribed fire, grazing, agriculture where it fits, forest work, invasive control, water management — those tools stay available. Headquarters and regional layers get thinner so more capacity sits on the refuge. Hunting and fishing access that is compatible with the refuge’s purpose gets priority. Partnerships with the neighbors get stronger because the birds do not stop at the boundary.

Ducks Unlimited’s CEO Adam Putnam put it plainly: the original mission was habitat for future generations, and the plan refocuses staff and resources on the waterfowl habitat the system was built to support. Other groups said the same thing in different words. The signal from the people who live next to these places is the same one we hear when a wet spring or a dry one decides how many pairs come back.

### Why this matters on the ground

National Wildlife Refuges are the largest dedicated network of land and water for fish, wildlife, and plants in the country — more than 570 units and roughly 95 million acres. Migratory birds, especially waterfowl, are written into the founding purposes of a lot of them. When those places lose staff, defer maintenance, or spend more time on process than on water levels and food, the birds notice before the reports do.

We run a sanctuary in rural Tennessee. Our birds are domestic. They do not migrate. But the habitat math is the same math the refuge system is being told to solve again. Water that stays in the right places long enough for plants to grow. Food that is there when the birds need it. Predators managed enough that the survivors can raise young. Infrastructure that does not fall apart between seasons. None of that happens by memo. It happens because someone is on the property with the right tools and the authority to use them.

The order does not invent new money. It does not magically fill every vacant biologist slot. What it does is make the priority explicit: field operations over layers, habitat work over drift, and the original purposes — waterfowl included — over whatever else has been competing for attention. That is the part that changes who can build and maintain the places the birds actually use.

### The closer

Rooted in Purpose is not a celebration of the status quo. The preface frames it as an honest assessment of where the system has drifted and where discipline is required. Success gets measured by what happens on the refuge for wildlife, not by how many meetings got held.

For anyone who keeps birds, manages water, or watches the migration, the useful question is simple. Did the people closest to the habitat get clearer marching orders and fewer obstacles, or did another report land on a shelf? The paper and the order say the first thing. The next few seasons will show whether the work follows.
