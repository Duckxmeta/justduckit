Tuesday afternoon in Bandera County, a justice of the peace put a hard line on what a sanctuary can keep when the ground no longer supports the claim.

Justice of the Peace Gina Grothues ordered We Care Wildlife Sanctuary in Lakehills to surrender horses, pigs, cows and other animals to the county. Exceptions stay for dogs, goats, one donkey, frogs and certain birds. The order bars the sanctuary from taking new animals for five years. Anything left on the place must be spayed or neutered. No breeding program. Carcasses still on the property get buried or removed. County law enforcement can supervise for ten years.

That is not a suggestion. It is a civil judgment after a two-day hearing on September 14 and 15. The hearing followed a September 4 search and seizure. Responders from the constable’s office, sheriff’s office, American Humane Society, and San Antonio Zoo veterinary staff documented more than 100 animals removed and more than 20 dead on the property, including roughly 15 horses and two primates in various stages of decomposition.

Veterinarians and animal welfare people testified about what they saw. Body condition scores low enough to trigger seizure. Carcasses left where they fell. The judge called the carcass requirement a health and safety issue. She said there will not be a breeding program there.

Owner Armando Mendez has called the seizure a setup. In earlier interviews he said most of the animals taken were turtles, tortoises and chickens, and that prior raids preceded court dates in an ongoing legal fight. No criminal charges have been reported in the coverage so far. The September order is civil: what happens to the animals, not a criminal conviction.

## The gap between the sign and the fence line

Anyone who has walked a property with animals knows the difference between a name on a gate and the daily math of feed, water, shade, and feet on the ground. A sanctuary is not a brand. It is the capacity to keep the animals you already have from dying on the property.

We run a place in rural Tennessee. The work is not abstract. Water freezes. Feed prices move. One sick bird can take a night. When the numbers exceed the systems, the systems do not scale by goodwill. They fail in ways that leave bodies and records that a judge can later read back in open court.

The Bandera case is not the first time a place calling itself a sanctuary has been ordered to stop. It is one more data point that the word does not protect the animals or the operator when the evidence shows basic husbandry did not hold. American Humane Society responders said they documented critical welfare failures and severe neglect. That language is specific. It is not “we disagree on philosophy.” It is “these animals did not have the minimums.”

## What the order actually changes

The five-year ban on new animals and the spay/neuter rule close the door on growth. The ten-year supervision clause keeps law enforcement in the picture. Remaining animals that stay must be altered. The exceptions (dogs, goats, one donkey, frogs, certain birds) are narrow. Everything else leaves.

For the animals already seized, they sit in temporary shelter while the civil process finishes. For the ones that stay, the order tries to stop the cycle that produced the carcasses. Whether that works depends on whether the remaining capacity matches the remaining animals. Court orders do not fill water troughs.

## Why a builder notices

If you ship software and also keep animals, you already know the version-control problem. The production environment is the actual pens, not the mission statement. When the production environment starts producing dead horses and primates, the rollback is not a git revert. It is a warrant and a hearing.

The owner’s claim that this is timed around other legal matters is a claim that has to be tested in its own venue. The photographs, the body scores, the necropsies on the primates, and the testimony of the vets and Humane Society people are the production logs. Those logs are what the judge used. A builder who has watched a system fail under load does not need a long essay to understand why a court would refuse to let the same load continue.

We do not get to declare ourselves the exception because the animals are “rescued.” The standard is whether they are alive, fed, watered, and not left to rot where they fell. When that standard is not met, the name on the gate does not matter.

The closer is the practical one. If you run a sanctuary or plan to, the Bandera order is a reminder that the limiting factor is not the logo. It is whether you can prove, every day, that the animals in your care are better off than they would have been without you. When the proof fails, the county will eventually do the arithmetic for you.
