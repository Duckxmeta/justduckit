Eighteen organizations from fourteen countries just stood up a six-year effort to keep three species of diving ducks from sliding further. The project is called Diving Ducks LIFE. It is funded under the European Union’s LIFE programme and coordinated by the Bulgarian Society for the Protection of Birds. The formal kick-off meeting ran 4–6 November in Sofia; public announcements from partner groups landed on 25 November.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="98" />

The three birds are the White-headed duck (*Oxyura leucocephala*), the Ferruginous duck (*Aythya nyroca*), and the Common pochard (*Aythya ferina*). All three are globally threatened. The work covers the Black Sea–Mediterranean Flyway. Partner countries include Bulgaria, Greece, Croatia, Romania, Kazakhstan, North Macedonia, Albania, Serbia, Montenegro, Türkiye, Uzbekistan, France, and the United Kingdom, with strategic cooperation further along the route including Azerbaijan. Collectively those places hold more than 90 percent of the White-headed duck’s Eastern Flyway wintering population and major shares of the other two species.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="99" />

## What the numbers actually say

The project runs from 1 September 2026 to 31 August 2032. Total eligible budget is listed at 12,589,086 euros, with an EU contribution of 9,441,815 euros. Targets: restore and rehabilitate more than 20,600 hectares of priority wetlands, and improve safety across 217,000 hectares of breeding, staging, and wintering habitat. Specific aims include cutting bycatch in fishing gear by 50 percent, cutting poaching incidents by 50 percent, and cutting disturbance from stray dogs by 50 percent at key sites. Pilot climate-smart restoration is meant to make the gains last.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="99" />

These are not abstract habitat goals. Diving ducks need open water deep enough to feed, clean edges for nesting, and stopover wetlands that still hold food when the birds arrive on migration. When those pieces degrade—drainage, pollution, abandoned nets, lead shot, illegal take, or dogs running the margins—the birds drop. The project treats the three species as umbrella species: improve conditions for them and a wider set of waterbirds and the wetlands themselves benefit.

## Why this is the work, not a care guide

Anyone who has run a small sanctuary knows the difference between one rescued bird and a system that keeps the next thousand from needing rescue. Local care handles the individuals that already fell through. Flyway-scale work is what keeps the wild populations from shrinking to the point that every remaining bird is one storm or one bad season away from the same problem.

The threats named in the project match what shows up in real wetlands: habitat loss, bycatch, lead poisoning, illegal killing, and disturbance. In Bulgaria the partners plan to work with the fisheries agency against illegal net fishing and to pull abandoned nets. In Croatia a partner is restoring a large wetland area inside Kopački Rit Nature Park. Other countries will focus on monitoring numbers, reducing poaching, or climate-smart restoration. The actions differ by country because the pressures differ; the coordination is the point.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="101" />

## What moved in the last day

The formal start was early November. What landed on 25 November were the coordinated public announcements from multiple partner organizations—Hellenic Ornithological Society, Bulgarian Society for the Protection of Birds, Romanian partners, and others—making the scope, the species, the hectare targets, and the partner list visible at the same time. That is the moment the project moved from internal kick-off to public record.

For a builder who also runs animal care, the practical read is simple. These three ducks are not backyard pets. They are migratory waterfowl whose wintering and breeding sites sit across national borders. The project is putting EU LIFE money and 18 organizations on the same problem set that smaller rescues see in pieces: bad water, missing cover, nets, dogs, and people who still take birds that are already scarce. The scale is the news.

## The closer

Twenty thousand hectares restored and two hundred thousand made safer is not a press-release number if the partners actually deliver the net pulls, the wetland work, and the monitoring. Diving ducks respond quickly when the water and the edges improve. That is the bet. The flyway is long. The partners are many. The next six years will show whether the coordination holds when the actual dirt and the actual nets are in the way.
