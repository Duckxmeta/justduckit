Delaware wildlife officials dropped the first highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) findings of the fall on November 20. The detections came from hunter-harvested ducks at Little Creek Wildlife Area in eastern Kent County, sampled during the opening stretch of the 2026-27 duck season (October 31 to November 7).<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="64" />

Seven birds across three species tested presumptive positive for H5 at the Pennsylvania Veterinary Laboratory: green-winged teal, American black duck, and northern shoveler. USDA-APHIS ran the routine surveillance for DNREC’s Division of Fish and Wildlife. Snow geese usually carry the biggest numbers here each winter, but this year’s first positives landed in ducks.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="67" />

The same day, the Delaware Department of Agriculture reported a separate presumptive positive in a commercial poultry flock in Sussex County. That property went under quarantine; the birds are being depopulated and will not enter the food supply. Two detections in one day is the kind of coincidence that makes people who keep birds check their gates twice.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="73" />

## What the numbers actually say

This is not a novel strain announcement or a human case cluster. CDC still rates the risk to the general public from H5 infections as low. Most human cases in recent years have been among people who work closely with farm animals. The signal is in the wild birds and the poultry side of the ledger.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="64" />

DNREC is asking hunters and the public to report groups of sick or dead wild birds—ducks, geese, shorebirds, raptors—through the Division of Fish and Wildlife form. A report does not guarantee a response team shows up. It feeds the tracking that tells biologists how hard the virus is hitting Delaware wildlife this season. The next waterfowl season segment opens November 25. That is the window where more samples will move.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="64" />

For residents who find a dead wild bird on their property and want to remove it: gloves, mask, safety glasses. Double-bag it, zip-tie the bag, put it in the trash for landfill pickup. Wash hands after. The virus spreads through nasal and eye secretions and manure. It moves on equipment, clothing, and boots between flocks.

## Why this lands on a sanctuary

We run ducks in rural Tennessee. Domestic birds and wild waterfowl share the same air and the same drainage when the weather turns. A positive in hunter-harvested teal a few states away does not mean our pens are compromised tomorrow. It does mean the seasonal pressure is back on the board.

Anyone who keeps waterfowl or poultry already knows the basic rules: limit contact between wild birds and the flock, change boots and clothes before moving between groups, keep feed and water from becoming a congregation point for migrants. The Delaware notice is the annual reminder that those rules are not optional when the migration is moving and the labs are finding H5 in the samples.

Hunters who also raise birds get the sharper version of the same advice. Do not harvest or handle obviously sick or dead wild birds. Wear rubber gloves when cleaning game. Wash tools and surfaces. Cook poultry to 165 °F internal. The protocols are short because the failure modes are short: one pair of boots, one shared trailer, one open gate during the wrong week.

## The commercial side and the wild side

The Sussex County commercial detection is the one that moves trucks and depopulation crews. The Kent County wild-bird detections are the ones that move the surveillance numbers and the hunter advisories. Both sit in the same virus family. Both make the case for treating wild waterfowl as a continuous environmental pressure rather than a one-off event.

Delaware has seen HPAI become a regular Mid-Atlantic winter feature, mostly on the snow goose flocks that drop in from the Arctic. Finding it first in teal, black ducks, and shovelers this year does not rewrite the playbook. It does confirm the virus is already circulating in the migratory waterfowl that use the same wetlands and fields.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="64" />

For a sanctuary that takes in domestic ducks, the practical distinction is simple. Wild birds carry the risk in. Domestic birds in pens can amplify it if the virus gets inside. The distance between those two facts is measured in boots, gates, and how seriously you treat the drainage and the feeders when the migration is on.

## What to watch next

The November 25 waterfowl opener will generate more samples. DNREC and USDA-APHIS will keep the routine surveillance running. Any additional commercial or backyard detections will show up through the Department of Agriculture channels. The public reporting form is the early-warning layer for groups of dead birds that do not get harvested.

If you run waterfowl, the work is already on the calendar: tighten the biosecurity that was already supposed to be tight, watch the drainage and the wild-bird traffic around the pens, and keep the contact information for your state vet or the local equivalent handy. The Delaware announcement is not a surprise. It is the first numbered data point of the season that says the virus is moving with the birds again.

We will keep the pens clean, the boots dedicated, and the wild-bird contact as low as the land allows. That is the job when the labs start returning H5 from the samples. Proof it on your own place.
