Delaware’s Division of Fish and Wildlife got the first highly pathogenic avian influenza hit of the fall this week. Seven hunter-harvested ducks from the opening segment of the 2026-27 duck season tested presumptive positive for H5 at the Pennsylvania Veterinary Laboratory. The samples came out of routine USDA-APHIS surveillance at Little Creek Wildlife Area in eastern Kent County.

The birds were green-winged teal, American black duck, and northern shoveler. Snow geese usually carry the biggest flocks and the highest attention in Delaware, but the first positives this season came from ducks. The next waterfowl season segment opens November 25.

This is not a backyard care tip. It is a live pathogen moving through the migration that feeds both wild populations and the commercial poultry industry that sits a short drive from most East Coast sanctuaries.

## What the numbers actually say

The detection window was narrow: the first segment of Delaware duck season ran October 31 through November 7. Hunters brought birds in, USDA-APHIS sampled them for DNREC, and the lab returned the presumptive H5 results. Officials have not yet released full sequencing or confirmed pathogenicity on every sample in the public notes, but the state treated the findings as the season’s first HPAI event and issued the standard hunter and public alerts.

CDC still rates risk to the general public from H5 infections as low. Most human cases in recent years have been agricultural or dairy workers with direct animal contact. That does not make the virus irrelevant to people who keep waterfowl or run rescues. Domestic ducks and geese are susceptible. So are the wild birds that fly over or land in the same ponds, fields, and drainage ditches our birds use.

Delaware’s poultry industry is dense. A commercial broiler farm in neighboring Worcester County, Maryland, had already confirmed H5N1 earlier in the month. The state Department of Agriculture had already pushed biosecurity reminders before this wild-bird detection. The two events sit in the same corridor.

## Practical steps for anyone who keeps birds

DNREC and the Delaware Department of Agriculture are telling hunters and the public the same short list:

- Do not harvest or handle birds that are obviously sick or found dead.
- Wear gloves when cleaning game or cleaning feeders.
- Wash hands, tools, and surfaces immediately afterward.
- Keep raw game separate from ready-to-eat food.
- Cook poultry to an internal temperature of 165°F.

Anyone who sees groups of sick or dead wild birds is asked to report through the Division of Fish and Wildlife sick-and-dead wildlife form instead of moving the carcasses.

For sanctuary or backyard keepers the operational translation is tighter. Keep domestic flocks away from open water and fields that migratory waterfowl use. Change boots and clothes between wild-bird areas and the pens. Limit visitor traffic during peak migration. Have a plan for what happens if a wild bird dies inside the fence or if a commercial operation nearby reports a case. Those are the same steps we already run when flu seasons get loud; the difference is that the surveillance just confirmed the virus is present in the ducks that are still moving south.

## Why this is a builder problem

Running a sanctuary is logistics plus biology. Feed schedules, water quality, predator exclusion, and vet relationships are the daily work. Pathogen pressure is the external variable that forces the schedule to change. When HPAI shows up in the migratory ducks two states away, the response is not a new protocol from scratch. It is tightening the gates, the footwear protocol, and the decision threshold for calling the state poultry hotline.

The same systems that keep track of which birds are in which pen, which ones are under quarantine, and which partners can take overflow become the difference between a contained event and a forced depopulation. That is why the reporting forms, the hunter guidance, and the commercial farm confirmations matter even if your birds never leave the property. The migration does not care about fence lines.

Delaware’s next waterfowl segment starts in five days. The snow geese that usually dominate the HPAI conversation in the state will keep arriving. The first positives this fall were already in three species of ducks. For anyone who keeps waterfowl or works next to people who do, the useful move is the same one the state is asking for: eyes open, hands clean, and a clear line between the wild birds and the ones under your care.
