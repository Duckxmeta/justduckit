More than 300 dead wild birds have turned up across five southern Michigan counties so far this year, and the bulk of the recent finds are Canada geese. Michigan DNR staff collected 77 dead Canada geese and one mallard from the Fennville Farm Unit at Allegan State Game Area. More than 80 dead Canada geese were reported along the Shiawassee River near Owosso. About 25 at Williamston Lakes in Ingham County. Another 20 along the Grand River in Grand Ledge, Eaton County. Roughly 100 in Washtenaw County.

Samples from the Allegan, Shiawassee, Williamston, and Washtenaw sites went to the Michigan State University Veterinary Diagnostics Laboratory. Preliminary results point to avian influenza. Anything that tests positive moves on to the USDA National Veterinary Services Laboratory in Ames for confirmation, a process that can take weeks. The DNR is treating HPAI as widespread in Michigan wild birds. Confirmed cases will land on their public page; absence of a confirmed listing in a county does not mean the virus is absent.

This is not a care-sheet reminder. It is a mortality event reported while waterfowl are moving south for the season. Fall migration packs birds into smaller patches of open water and food. When a highly pathogenic strain is already circulating, those concentrations turn into die-offs. The DNR does not expect statewide population crashes for ducks, geese, or swans. Local groups near the hot spots can take a hit. Bald eagles, terns, and cormorants have already shown up in earlier years of this outbreak. The same virus that knocks down a flock of geese on a river can reach raptors that scavenge the carcasses.

## What the numbers actually show

The DNR has been watching HPAI in Michigan wildlife since the 2022 outbreak. The pattern shifts year to year. This season’s visible signal is geese in the southern Lower Peninsula. Staff are prioritizing large mortality events, raptors, and abnormal-acting hawks, owls, and wild mammals for sampling. They are not asking the public to bag every dead bird. If a carcass is hard to reach or sits where people and domestic animals will not contact it, leave it. The virus dies as the bird decomposes. If you do have to move one, gloves, mask, eye protection, and double-bagging for the trash are the minimum. Property owners handle cleanup on private land. The agency samples; it does not run a disposal service.

Report die-offs of six or more wild birds through Michigan.gov/EyesInTheField or your local DNR office. The same tool covers sick-looking birds. Hunters still have Canada goose opportunities at the Fennville unit, with the usual precautions: do not handle obviously sick or dead birds, dress game away from poultry, let gear dry between trips. Backyard flocks and commercial poultry remain under MDARD biosecurity guidance. Cats have tested positive in prior years; keep them inside. Dogs have not shown domestic cases in the U.S. so far, but contact with sick or dead wildlife is still a bad idea.

## Why a sanctuary operator cares about a Michigan river

We run birds in Tennessee. The river in question is not ours. The movement is. Migratory waterfowl do not respect state lines, and HPAI has been endemic in North American wild birds for years. A die-off reported in Allegan or Washtenaw is a data point on the same map that covers our flyway. Sanctuaries that take in waterfowl, or that sit near wetlands where wild birds stage, watch these reports the way a builder watches an upstream outage. You cannot patch the virus. You can tighten the perimeter, decide whether new intakes get tested or held off-site, and keep the reporting channel open so the next cluster does not land only on social media.

The practical layer is small and physical. Feeders cleaned every two weeks with a bleach solution. No seed on the ground that pulls larger groups together. Domestic birds kept from free contact with wild ones. The reporting layer is digital and public. Eyes in the Field is a form, not a dashboard, but it is the intake that feeds the sampling. When preliminary results come back positive and the confirmatory lab is still weeks out, the public number is still “suspected.” That lag is normal. It is also the window where people decide whether to ignore the report or treat every dead goose as a potential signal.

## The builder’s version of the same problem

Running a sanctuary is physical ops with a long feedback loop. You see the birds every day. You do not see the lab result for weeks. The tools that close that loop are mostly state and federal: sampling protocols, confirmatory labs, public dashboards that lag. The part a small operation can own is the local signal—what shows up on the property, what gets reported, what gets isolated. That is closer to infrastructure monitoring than to a how-to list. You instrument the perimeter you control, you log the anomalies, and you do not wait for a perfect confirmed case before you change behavior.

Michigan’s numbers are specific and recent. More than 300 birds across five counties, heavy on Canada geese, preliminary avian influenza. The DNR expects continued cases because the virus is already widespread. Local declines near the sites are possible; statewide populations are not the concern they are tracking. For anyone whose work includes live waterfowl, the useful part is the same as any other system: the signal arrived, the confirmation will lag, and the response that matters is the one you can execute with the birds and the tools you already have.

We will keep watching the same channels. The geese on the Shiawassee are not our geese. The pattern is.
