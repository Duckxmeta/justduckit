To the casual observer, a duck’s head looks like a sleek, aerodynamic unit designed for diving and dabbling. There are eyes, a bill, and plenty of feathers — but no visible ears. This has led to the common misconception that ducks have poor hearing or lack ears entirely.

The truth is that ducks have exceptional hearing. They have simply mastered the art of the hidden ear.

### 1. Where Are the Ears?

If you gently part the feathers just behind and slightly below a duck’s eye, you will find a small open hole — the external acoustic meatus, the entrance to the ear canal.

Ducks lack a pinna (the fleshy external ear flap that mammals have). An external flap would create drag in flight and constant water resistance during dives. Instead, the shape of the head helps direct sound toward those small openings.

### 2. The Protective Shield: Auricular Feathers

The ears stay hidden because of specialized feathers called auriculars. These are fine and lacy rather than thick and stiff. They serve two important purposes:

- They shield the ear canal from wind pressure during flight and from water when the duck submerges its head.
- They remain acoustically transparent, allowing sound waves to pass through with very little interference — similar to the mesh on a speaker.

### 3. How Well Do They Hear?

Ducks are finely tuned to the frequencies of their own species and common predators.

- Despite having no external flaps, they have strong directional hearing. Their brains use the tiny difference in when a sound reaches each ear to locate the source.
- While humans have three middle-ear bones, ducks have a single efficient bone called the columella that transfers sound from the eardrum to the inner ear.

### 4. Hearing in Air and Underwater

- **In air**: Hearing is critical for communication. Ducklings begin listening to their mother’s calls while still inside the egg, which supports imprinting right after hatching.
- **Underwater**: They do not hear exactly the same way as on land, but they can detect vibrations and pressure changes that help them navigate and avoid threats while diving.

### 5. Why This Matters at a Sanctuary

Understanding duck hearing changes how we manage them:

- Sudden loud noises (machinery, dogs, etc.) are more stressful than many people realize. A quieter environment is a healthier one.
- Ear problems are uncommon but can occur, often secondary to respiratory issues or poor water quality. Excessive head scratching or a persistent head tilt is worth investigating.

Ducks may hide their ears, but they are not missing much. The next time you see a bird tilt its head, it is often listening as carefully as it is looking.
