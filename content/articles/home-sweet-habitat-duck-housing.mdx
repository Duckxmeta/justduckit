Ducks are surprisingly hardy creatures, but they are also messy, wet, and unfortunately delicious to almost every predator in North America. Creating the perfect duck habitat isn’t about building a miniature mansion — it’s about balancing security, ventilation, and water access.

### 1. The “Duck Den”: Basic Housing Requirements

Ducks prefer a simple, ground-level house they can easily waddle in and out of.

- **Space**: Aim for at least 4 square feet per bird inside the coop and 10–15 square feet per bird in the outdoor run.
- **Access Doors**: Make them wide enough for two ducks to pass at once. Narrow doorways stress them.
- **Ventilation**: Place vents high (above the birds’ heads) so moisture and ammonia can escape without creating cold drafts.
- **Lighting**: A secure window helps regulate their circadian rhythms and can support egg production in darker months.

### 2. Interior Furnishings

- **Bedding**: Pine shavings or straw work best. Many people use the deep litter method. Avoid cedar (the oils can be toxic).
- **Nests**: Ducks prefer dark, quiet corners on the floor. A simple wooden frame or plastic tub on its side is enough — no elevated boxes needed.
- **Feeders**: Keep them outside the sleeping area when possible to reduce moisture and mess inside the coop.

### 3. The Maximum Security Perimeter

Ducks have almost no natural defenses.

- **Double-action latches**: Raccoons can open simple bolts. Use latches that require two distinct movements.
- **Apron fencing**: Extend hardware cloth 12–24 inches outward along the ground to stop digging predators.
- **Overhead protection**: Use aviary netting or a grid of high-tensile line to deter hawks and owls.
- **Important**: Never rely on chicken wire for security. Always use ½-inch hardware cloth.

### 4. The Water Debate: Ponds vs. Pools

Ducks need enough water to submerge their heads and wash their feathers, but a full pond is optional.

- A plastic kiddie pool is often the most practical solution — cheap, easy to clean, and deep enough for proper bathing.
- Keep ducks away from decorative koi ponds. They will eat the fish and destroy the plants.
- A secure dry coop at night is safer than leaving ducks on a pond, where they remain vulnerable to turtles and owls.

### 5. Moving Day: Introducing Ducks to Their New Home

- Keep new birds in the secure coop and run for 2–3 days so they learn where home and food are.
- Establish a consistent evening routine with a call and a small treat.
- Supervise the first free-ranging sessions so you can spot any fencing weak points.

Start with strong security and good ventilation, then adjust based on how your flock actually uses the space. A well-designed habitat will keep them safe and comfortable for years.
