Thirteen birds did not make it out of the oil. The rest are back in their pens.

Broughshane Waterfowl Park in County Antrim shut down after a home heating oil tank was vandalised near the Main Street. About 200 litres of kerosene leaked, reached the pond through the drainage after overnight flooding, and coated the birds. The Northern Ireland Environment Agency got the call on 22 October. They ran absorbent booms and pulled the bulk of it out. The park has since reopened. On Monday morning, roughly ten to twelve birds returned, including two black swans and the Nenes.

Gary Hayward, chairman of the Broughshane Environment Wildfowl and Wildlife Trust, said the returning birds are lucky to be healthy. Some oil still sits along the edges of the pens. Once that is gone, the rest move back. More than twenty birds had already gone to the USPCA headquarters for specialist care. Every one of them needed bathing. Geese, ducks, and swans came in coated. Some had already ingested enough that treatment came too late. The park thanked the USPCA and the public. Enquiries are still open.

We run a sanctuary in rural Tennessee. We know what it looks like when something external hits the water and the birds cannot just fly off. Heating oil is not a predator. It does not leave tracks. It sits on the surface, soaks into feathers, and turns a normal preening session into a toxin delivery system. Birds hide illness better than most of us hide a bad day. By the time you see the signs, the damage is already inside.

## What the numbers actually mean

The park holds about 120 collection birds plus whatever wild ones drop in. Losing thirteen is not a rounding error. Two Trumpeter swans and more than ten ducks were already confirmed gone before the black swan count rose. The USPCA washed and treated what they could and dosed some with charcoal. Non-native species stay in care until the pond is certified clean. You cannot release a bird back into residual kerosene and call it a win.

Hayward noted that the NIEA made the problem smaller than it could have been. That is the practical measure. Not zero oil. Not a pristine pond by Tuesday. Less oil than the flood and the vandalism would have left on their own. The booms and the removal work bought the window for the survivors to come home.

For anyone who keeps waterfowl or helps run a place that does, the sequence is familiar. First the call. Then the count of who is still standing. Then the triage of who can be cleaned on site versus who needs a facility that can handle oil-soaked plumage and internal exposure. Then the slow wait while the water itself is brought back to something the birds can live in. None of that is optional. None of it scales if the people doing the work are already stretched thin.

## Why this is a builder problem

Sanctuaries and small waterfowl collections live on the same thin margin we do. Feed, bedding, vet runs, and the occasional emergency. A single tank of heating oil, opened by someone who wanted the fuel and did not care where the rest went, becomes a multi-week recovery. The Trust had already dealt with flooding the night before. The oil rode the drainage in. Contingency is not a document. It is extra hands, extra absorbent material, and a relationship with the local wildlife hospital that already knows your birds by name.

We have watched similar stories from the feed store and the fairgrounds. Someone’s pond gets hit by runoff, or a spill from a nearby property, or a predator that finds a weak fence. The public sees the recovery post and the thank-you. They do not see the nights spent checking the remaining birds for secondary infections or the cost of replacing contaminated substrate. The Broughshane crew got the birds home because the USPCA and the NIEA stayed on it. That is not automatic. It is people who answered the phone.

Digital tools do not clean feathers. They do, however, keep the story moving when the pens are still half-closed and the volunteers are still on the water. A short update that says the birds are back, the oil is mostly gone, and the public can visit again is how a small place stays visible after the worst week. Hayward’s quotes were plain. The park’s social post was plain. That is the version that travels.

## The edge that remains

Police still want information. The tank was damaged at a business premise. The exact path from vandalism to drainage is documented. What is not yet public is who did it and whether any of the oil was taken. For the birds that died, the distinction does not matter. For the people who run the park, the open enquiry is the remaining variable.

The returning birds are the measurable part. Ten to twelve back in pens. Two black swans and Nenes among them. The rest still waiting on the last edge oil to clear. Temporary pens still out of bounds. That is a recovery, not a reset. Anyone who has rebuilt a coop after a storm or a predator night knows the difference. The structure is standing. The birds are present. The work of making the place fully usable again continues.

We do not need another list of how to clean an oil-soaked duck. We need the reminder that the places holding these birds are one bad night away from needing outside help, and that the help that showed up in County Antrim is what turned a closed park into one that reopened with birds in it. The numbers are small. The work is not.

The birds came back. The oil did not take the whole flock. That is the fact that sits on the page this morning.
