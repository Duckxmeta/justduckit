Carolina Waterfowl Rescue got the call on October 2. By the next day, more than two dozen staff and volunteers had trailers, crates, and emergency kits on the road to a half-acre property in Harmony, North Carolina.

What they found was a hoarding case: chickens, Muscovy ducks, geese, pigeons, turkeys, and a few sheep left without food or water. Some had already died. Survivors were starving and, in places, feeding on the remains of flockmates. Executive Director Jennifer Gordon later described the scene as one of the worst she had seen in six or seven years.

The numbers she provided afterward were specific. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue took in 113 Muscovy ducks, 106 chickens, 59 pigeons, 11 geese, two turkeys, two doves, and three sheep. Other reports put the total birds removed above 800. Iredell County Animal Services confirmed the animals came from a Windmill Court property. The investigation is ongoing. No charges had been filed as of the latest public statements.

This is not a backyard flock that got behind on feed. This is the kind of sudden intake that breaks a sanctuary’s capacity before the first medical chart is finished.

## What the intake actually looks like

Gordon’s team spent the evening of the second prepping. They left early Saturday, drove more than an hour, and stayed until late afternoon once every animal was off the property. Birds that were medically stable enough received numbered bands and charts. Others went for further testing.

The immediate problem is housing and feed. A rescue already carrying hundreds of resident animals does not absorb 100-plus Muscovies and another 100 chickens without rearranging pens, stretching feed budgets, and calling in help. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue posted public appeals for donations, adoptions, and partner sanctuaries willing to take birds once they clear medical. Their site still carries the banner: hundreds need help; CWR is answering the call.

One detail that stuck with us: Gordon said there was not a bowl of water anywhere on the property and no food. The animals that could still move were sitting at people’s feet once rescuers arrived, begging. That is the moment the work shifts from finding the animals to keeping them alive long enough for the rest of the system to catch up.

## Why this lands harder for anyone who already runs animals

We run a sanctuary in rural Tennessee. We know what it costs to take in even a handful of dumped domestic ducks. Domestic waterfowl do not migrate. They do not survive a Tennessee or Carolina winter on their own once someone stops feeding them. When a property is abandoned or overwhelmed, the birds that remain are almost always the ones that cannot leave.

A single large intake like this forces the same tradeoffs we face on a smaller scale: which pens can be cleared, how much feed is left in the shed, whether the laundry can keep up with the bedding, and whether the next vet bill arrives before the donation that covers it. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue is also dealing with an unrelated practical bottleneck—commercial laundry capacity. Staff have been hauling loads to a laundromat while they raise money for on-site washers that can handle 80-to-120-pound loads. The everyday math does not pause because a hoarding case just landed.

The previous owners of the Iredell property were reported deported, leaving a teenage daughter to manage the animals. She became overwhelmed. That detail is not an excuse. It is a reminder that the animals do not care about the paperwork or the border status. They still need water, feed, and somewhere dry to stand.

## What moves next

Adoptions will matter. Gordon has said adopters will be vital once birds are medically cleared. Partner rescues that can take birds reduce the pressure on one facility. Donations cover the unexpected feed, veterinary, and housing costs that do not show up in a normal monthly budget.

For anyone who keeps ducks or runs a sanctuary, the practical takeaway is simple. Capacity is not theoretical. When a county animal services office calls a waterfowl rescue instead of trying to place the birds themselves, it is because the numbers already exceed what local shelters can hold. The work then falls on the groups that have the trailers, the experience with waterfowl, and the willingness to say yes before they have a place for every bird.

Carolina Waterfowl Rescue said yes. The birds are off the property. The next weeks will show whether the rest of the system can keep them there without collapsing the rescue that took them in.

If you can house birds or write a check, their site is open. The rest of us who already have animals know the math. It does not get easier just because the call came from another county.
