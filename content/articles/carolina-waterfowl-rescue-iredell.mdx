Carolina Waterfowl Rescue got the call on October 2. By the next afternoon they had pulled every animal off a half-acre property in Harmony, Iredell County. Jennifer Gordon, the executive director, later listed what came in: 113 Muscovy ducks, 106 chickens, 59 pigeons, 11 geese, two turkeys, two doves, and three sheep. Roughly 296 animals that left with no bowls of water and no feed in sight.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="59" />

Other early reports from the same week put the total higher, over 800 birds in severe distress, with some already dead and survivors eating remains. Numbers in these cases move as animals are counted, triaged, and sometimes transferred. The consistent part is the condition and the organization that answered. Gordon told Queen City News the scene was among the worst she had seen in six or seven years. Animals were packed tight. A teenage daughter had been left with the property after the previous owners were deported. She was overwhelmed. The birds were not.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="61" />

## What the call actually costs

Over two dozen staff and volunteers rolled trailers, crates, and emergency kits more than an hour north. They stayed until every animal was off the ground. Each bird gets a numbered band and a chart. The ones that need more than food and quiet go to clinic. The rest need space that the sanctuary did not already have empty.

That is the part most people miss when they watch a rescue video. The day of the pull is loud. The weeks after are feed, meds, laundry, and deciding which birds can go to homes and which stay. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue is a registered sanctuary that already runs permanent residents alongside rehab. Taking this many domestic waterfowl and poultry at once means temporary pens, extra feed runs, and staff time that was already spoken for. Their site still has the banner up asking for help to make room. Donations go to food, medical, supplies, and housing while they keep the rest of the flock fed.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="64" />

## Why this one lands

Domestic ducks and geese do not migrate. A Muscovy that spent its life on a yard does not suddenly know how to evade predators or find clean water once the bowls are gone. Dumping or neglect does not turn them wild. It turns them into the next round of sanctuary intake. We see the same pattern here. Someone starts with a few birds, numbers climb, care stops, and the call goes out. The difference this week is scale and the fact that a dedicated waterfowl rescue was the one that answered.

Gordon has said the animals were overwhelmed and starving. That matches what the photos show: mixed flocks of ducks, geese, and chickens under makeshift shelter, tires and scrap around them, no visible feed or water. Some reports note animals that had already died. The survivors now sit in the system that exists for exactly this: a place that will keep them instead of releasing them back into the same situation.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="60" />

## The builder side of sanctuary work

Anyone who runs a physical operation knows the moment the unexpected load hits. You already have the daily list. Then the phone rings and the list doubles. Trailers get prepped the night before. People cancel other plans. You count crates and hope the feed store is open early. After the animals are in, the accounting starts: how many more pounds of pellets, how many extra vet visits, which enclosures can be extended without creating new problems.

That is the same constraint any builder faces when the real world does not match the plan. Code can scale on a server. Birds need square footage, clean water, and someone who will open the gate at dawn. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue is doing the version of that work that does not ship with a dashboard. Their public ask is practical: adoptions for birds that can leave, donations for the ones that stay, and supplies that keep the laundry and the feeders running. The investigation in Iredell County is still open. No charges had been filed as of the latest local reporting.<grok type="render_inline_citation" citation_id="59" />

## What comes next for the birds

The ones that stabilize will be sorted. Some will go to new homes through the rescue’s adoption list. Others will stay as permanent residents if their condition or history makes rehoming the wrong call. Permanent residents are the part of sanctuary work that does not end when the news cycle moves. They still need feed, shelter, and predator protection every day.

For anyone watching from outside, the useful move is the same one the rescue is already asking for. Check their adoption page if you can take birds. Send feed or money if you cannot. The half-acre property in Harmony is empty of animals now. The capacity problem moved with them.

We keep our own gates because the next call is never on the calendar. When it comes, the difference is whether the place on the other end of the phone still has room.
