There was not a bowl of water anywhere on the property. No food. Half an acre in Iredell County packed with chickens, ducks, geese, turkeys, pigeons, and a few sheep. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue got the call.

Jennifer Gordon, their executive director, told local stations it was probably the worst she had seen in six or seven years. Not the largest. The worst. Birds running loose and scared. Some already dead. Survivors scavenging scraps, and in at least one report, feeding on flockmates. The exact headcount depends on which story you read. Early coverage said more than 800 animals on the property. One later local piece said Carolina Waterfowl Rescue itself took in 296: 113 Muscovy ducks, 106 chickens, 59 pigeons, 11 geese, two turkeys, two doves, and three sheep from a house on Windmill Court in Harmony. The rest went to other groups or did not make it. The rescue is still asking for room, adoptions, and cash because the ones they have are already more than the current setup was built for.

## What the floor looks like when the numbers arrive

We know what a sudden influx does. You clear the hospital pens first. You band every bird so you can tell them apart when the vet notes start stacking. You check feet, bills, keels, eyes. You pull the ones that cannot stand. You put the rest somewhere they can drink without fighting. Then you start the math on feed, bedding, and who can take a bachelor flock of roosters or a group of Muscovies that have never lived with people who actually feed them on time.

Carolina Waterfowl Rescue has been posting about bachelor roosters looking for homes. One of them, Terry, will climb into a lap. After what these birds just came out of, that is not cute. That is a bird that has not been touched kindly in a long time. Every rooster that leaves frees a pen. Every pen that opens is space for the next one that has not been found yet.

The site is still running the Iredell call for help next to their regular adoption page. Local stations in the Charlotte market and across the Carolinas ran the story again this week. The animals are already off the property. The work is the part that does not fit in a 90-second clip.

## Why this one landed here

This is not a care guide. This is what happens when the numbers outrun the pens. Domestic ducks and geese do not migrate. They do not go feral and thrive. When they are packed onto a half-acre with no water, the ones that survive the first days still need somewhere to stand that is not a rescue already running at capacity. Carolina Waterfowl Rescue is doing the part most people never see: the bands, the charts, the calls to other groups, the wish lists for feed, and the quiet work of getting a Muscovy or a goose to a place where someone will keep the water bowls filled.

We have watched the same pattern in Tennessee. One dump leads to the next. The sanctuary that takes the first wave is the one that has to say no to the second unless the first wave moves. Adoptions are the only real expansion. Donations cover the feed and the meds while the adoptions catch up. Neither is optional when the intake is this size.

## What is still open

If you can take a bird, start with the roosters. The hens are easier to place. The roosters are the ones still sitting. If you cannot take one, the Amazon and Chewy wish lists are public. So is the donation page. The rescue has been clear that the unexpected costs of a case this size hit while they are still feeding the animals they already had.

The property is cleared. The birds are not. Some of them are already looking for the next human who will not leave the water bowl empty.
